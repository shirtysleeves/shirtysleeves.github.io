The further I trudge or stagger along my sixth decade as a user-cum-vehicle of natural human language (for I am not singling out the English language for criticism), the more frustrated and disheartened I am by its cussed imprecision, as evidenced notably by my ever-increasing impulsion to recast even the simplest of sentences in my own speech and writing. I am not thinking here solely or even mainly of the notorious fuzziness of polysyllabic abstract nouns like Unbefangenheit or obnubilability, for some of the simplest concrete nouns seem to number among the worst offenders against clearcut-ness. The original opening sentence of the present sentence contains a case in point. At first, I intended this sentence to read, "I am a non-driver" or "I am a full-time pedestrian." But on reflection, I gathered that such wording would imply by default that I had never learned to drive, which is false. Then I thought of recasting it as "I am an ex-driver," but on reflection I gathered that such wording would imply that I could count on never driving again under any circumstances, which is also false--at least so far, for if I should happen never to drive a car between the present day and the day of my death, I shall at that expiring moment have proved myself to have been an ex-driver. In point of fact it would seem that an adequately ample and precise statement of my meta-automotive state is going to have to be quite wordy indeed and to read roughly (for even now I am not quite sure I have quite clinched it) as follows: I learned to drive a car back in 1988 and regularly drove one between that year and 1994, and as I presumably still know how to drive one, I am still a driver, although the last time I drove one (actually a van, for the very first [and, it is to be hoped, last] time) was in 1997, and I have done everything in my power to avoid driving one ever since. But the truth is that I have never been an enthusiastic driver and that I was averse to the notion of driving even before I started. In my sophomore year of high school, when my newly licensed classmates were rushing to get their "hang tags" authorizing them to park their newly acquired vehicles in the school's parking lot, I was content to continue riding the yellow bus to and from the campus, and it was only at my parents' insistence that I enrolled in driver's education class in the autumn of the following year, and began driving their massive powder-blue 1979 Ford station-wagon at a crawling pace around the neighborhood with my father in shotgun position. Howbeit, late in the spring semester I dutifully took and passed the driver's test, walleted my license, and began commuting to school under my own aurigational power on the first day of my senior year. And I remained a near daily active driver into my college-graduation year of 1994. But even by then, driving had failed to become second nature to me. Indeed, I can frankly and candidly say that I retain only one genuinely happy memory from my entire five-year history as a regular driver; a memory in which driving figures as that totem or symbol of complacent, easy-going freedom--the freedom of "the wide open road"--as which it proverbially figures in the imaginations and desire-scapes of twentieth and twentieth-century Americans--namely, my memory of those weekday late autumnal-to-early spring evenings of 1989-1990 when, in consequence of my admission to Hillsborough County's high-school academic quiz team, I was obliged to commute to and from my house in Keystone, in the northwest extremity of the county, to King High School in Temple Terrace some twenty miles to the east-southeast, where the team had its weekly (or possibly biweekly) coaching sessions. That easy descent along Gunn Highway and Busch Boulevard, into which Gunn debouched as its principal tributary, afforded me the most soothing possible combination of driving conditions--a moderate speed limit, few if any sharp turns, judiciously spaced traffic intersections, mercifully unglaring natural light nicely abetted by its artificial helpmeet (a succession of proper towering sodium-vapor streetlights, to whose orangish-yellow aura that of the setting sun seemed to yield at an imperceptibly lackadaisical pace and with the most complaisantly ingratiating good grace). Otherwise my driver's memory-scape is a farrago of barely missed sideswipes, bone-rattling sudden stops, and terrifyingly unpunctuated lunges along double-dozen stretches of multi-lane interstate--and always in one of two of the most harrowing and inhospitable natural settings--a blindingly sunlit midday exposing every potential hazard to view with cruel hyper-realism, and a torrential later-afternoon or early evening thunderstorm. I own that my brief driving career might have been substantially less "traumatic" had its principal vehicle (in both literal and figurative senses, natch) not been not the aforementioned Ford station-wagon but a pea-green 1981 Dodge Aries K Car that my parents purchased from some nuns for an-even-then unprincely sum of $500 and entrusted to me a few weeks into my high-school senior year. Compared with the station-wagon, which moved along the road with the stately assurance of a steel blue whale (yes, yes, yes: not unlike the Chrysler in the pop song), the Aries felt almost like a toy car, not only because it was much smaller than its predecessor but also because it seemed to have a much larger share of plastic in its composition. If I had then known about East Germany's notoriously flimsy and breakdown-prone Trabant compacts, I would doubtless have humorously likened it to one of those cars, for its poor ergonomics was or were but the least of its many shortcomings. It accelerated from idle to first gear (of its own immediate volition, for, like the station-wagon's, its transmission was automatic, and I have in fact never learned to drive a manual-transmission vehicle [I mention this experimentally,as it were; which is to say out of curiosity about the reader's reaction to it, for whereas it seemed to me in my day that there was no stigma whatsoever attached to driving automatic, it seems to me in this day that learning to drive "stick shift" is almost mandatory, and that automatic-only drivers are regarded as well-nigh subhuman gimps--but I own I could have been mistaken then and could be mistaken now]) with slug-like sluggishness, and my doubtless pointless efforts to compensate for this failing by slamming on the gas pedal at red-to-green intersection moments drove me headlong (or, rather, rearlong) into another defect--viz., its exhaust system's tendency to belch (or fart) great plumes of black smoke when given too much gas at once. I own that, what with traffic intersections being outdoor places and my being well to the front of the discharge, this defect did not impinge on my immediate comfort in the slightest. But I felt such an acute mixture of pity and fear on the score of the driver immediately behind me that the necessity of getting up to speed in time to re-join the flow of traffic was perhaps the smallest portion of my sorrows in the immediate post-green-lighted moments. Perhaps, I would think, the poor bastard or devil will rear-end me out of either spite a or a sheer cussed inability to make out the distance between his vehicle and mind amid all the fumage? And then, once I had gotten going, especially if the road-stretch of those moments was more than one-laned in each direction (and for some reason I picture it by default as the four or six-lane stretch of US 41, the so-called Tamiami Trail, straddling the border between Sarasota and Manatee Counties) I would worry that the driver would pull level with me and spew out a torrent of abuse through his rolled-down window, or even worse, open fire on me therefrom with the proverbial twelve-gauge shotgun (not that I knew then or know now exactly what sort of firearm a twelve-gauge shotgun is, or how frequently it actually figures in glove-compartment arsenals) in an access of road rage (not that I would then have called it road rage, for I distinctly remember that I first heard the term from my college friend Matt "Panino" McKinzie during one of his several visits to me in Baltimore, the first of which did not occur until the spring of 1995). And of course because one is required to stop and start at and from traffic intersections several times in even the briefest of drives, I was rarely free of black smoke-occasioned anxiety entirely; the retrospective fear occasioned by the most most recent discharge of fumage very quickly yielded to anticipatory fear of what peril the next discharge would actuate at the intersection immediately ahead. Another big problem with the car was that its engine was ridiculously prone to overheating, such that, for example, one found it effectively impossible to run the air-conditioning without flooding the interior of the vehicle with the sickly sweet smell maple-syrup odor of freon engine coolant. On regular urban and suburban streets this potentially fatal inundation of a seriously toxic gas (if I recollect aright, my father once said that being heavier than air, freon accumulated in the lungs, such that one might effectively drown from the inhalation of it [here, as in the case of so many of the hazardous phenomena inhabiting my memory-scape, the intensity and durability of my fear of the phenomenon is so acute as to render the actuality of the hazard and the probability of its supervention almost irrelevant]) could be reliably if none too swiftly checked by simply switching off the air conditioner (such that I quickly came essentially to do without climate control entirely, a sacrifice that I, having grown up in an un-airconditioned central-Floridian house, found easy enough to bear enough on my lonesome [apart, that is, from its epiphenomenal production of boreal white noise through the permanently rolled-down windows, which white noise tended to drown out all every decibel of sound emitted even at full volume from the speakers of my chickenshit 10-watt Sparkomatic stereo {does not the very brand-name Sparkomatic suggest an insouciantly juvenile lack of any interest in competing with the big boys of the automotive high-fidelity industry, your Panasonics and Boses and Bang-and-Olofsons (it was not for nothing that that one of the most memorable passengers minutes (and had my then-trusty high-school chum Jason Robertshaw not fortunately been riding shotgun and providing me with rock-solid roadside counsel all along the way, I might very well have remained stranded for days somewhere in the near-veritable no-man's land between Bradenton and Brandon). For the two-and-a-half year balance of my college pseudo-career, I circumvented this difficulty by undertaking the journey via the liberally spotlighted US 301 or the above-mentioned US 41, but when it came time for me to move to Baltimore in August of 1994, there could be no question of taking the Aries along, as the thousand-mile distance would have to be traversed largely via Interstate (although I do recall that when we visited my uncle in Jacksonville earlier that summer, he jestingly offered to help my father and me with the move by "bringing up the rear in the Aries"--an eloquent testimonial of that rattletrap's lowercase dodginess, for the man had never actually seen the car and therefore could only have learned of its unserviceability from my father, who would not have told him about it had he not thought of it as something even more noteworthy than something to write home about--i.e., something to report during a long-distance telephone call [Jacksonville being more than far enough away from Keystone-Odessa to count as long distance, and the summer of '94 occurring when long-distance telephony, although not nearly as expensive as it had been earlier in the century, still cost one several pretty pennies per minute {for I remember that when, in the fourth or fifth hour of a phone-chinwag with the above-mentioned "Panino" that took place during that selfsame summer, I gently suggested our "letting each other go" on account of the presumptive massiveness of the phone-toll (a toll that was destined to be charged to me via my parents (for I had been talking to him all the while via their bedroom "land line," I then not even having a terrestrial phone of my own in my bedroom, he gently opined that the charge could only amount to about twenty dollars}]). And as my parents had not mooted the idea of finding a replacement for the Aries, I was more than fully prepared for at least having a go at living carless in Baltimore, a go to which I was looking forward with considerable future-perfect relief, for since returning from Sarasota after graduation in late May, I had scarcely driven at all, my native apprehensions about the basal driving experience having by then been overlaid with a kind of paranoia catalyzed by intelligence of certain para-automotive phenomena of the larger world. You see, at some point in my last year at college, I had heard on National Public Radio's All Things Considered a report on and from one of those chaotically burgeoning sub-Saharan African capitals--Kinshasa, perhaps, or Nairobi. The report centered (or "focused like a laser" in the Clintonian patois of the micro-epoch) on a specific public-safety nuisance that had very recently come to the fore there--viz., that of streetside banditti threatening momentarily stationary drivers at gunpoint, expelling them from their cars, and then driving off in the vehicles. The reporter reported on this phenomenon in an attitude of vicarious bemusement, conveying the sense that even the locals regarded it as unprecedentedly barbaric there and thereby intimating that it was super-unprecedented here in the so-called developed world. But then only six-months at most later I learned from a local Tampa Bay-area evening television news broadcast of a recent local upsurge in car-jacking, a word then entirely new to me. It took me rather a long time to work out that the malefaction in question was not an exact automotive analogue to hijacking--viz., the mere ordering of a driver to take one to such-and-such a place--but rather an exact duplicate of the malefaction all-too-recently newly witnessed in Kinshasa or Nairobi. And this mimeography lent an ominously uncanny aura to car-jacking. Had I first heard of car-jacking via the local news report without having previously heard the NPR report, I would doubtless have been alarmed, but alarmed in a familiar, almost gemütlich fashion; the same fashion in which I had greeted the news of upticks in various sorts of well-known crimes over the previous decade or so--the news that, for example, "a murder day" was committed in the city of Tampa, a statistic polemically cited in 1986 by the city's then-mayor Bob Martinez's Democratic opponent in the gubernatorial election (which Martinez had subsequently won, doubtless in no small part because unlike my credulous then fourteen-year-old self, the voters of Florida had known darn well that the figure was wildly overblown, Tampa then having a homicide rate closer to a murder a week). But the seeming fact that car-jacking had been all but unknown to the point of even lacking a name in the so-called developing world and was already epidemic in such a relatively sleepy Occidental conurbation as Tampa Bay suggested that something was badly out of joint in the automotive realm, that driving had suddenly become a much more dangerous activity--and who knew how much more dangerous, or in what ways it would be, six months or a year thence? And so my driving hours were thenceforth blighted by an altogether new feeling of paranoia, such that I could not refrain from constantly glancing at the rear-view mirror to see if I was being followed by a potential car-jacker, or, indeed, someone who had it in for me personally (I am, incidentally, tarrying so long in the present digression partly out of buck- naked self-interest, which is to say by way of furnishing further proof that I am a person who simply has no business being behind the wheel of a car [although I own that were I to be forced to take up driving again at gunpoint à la a car-jackee, this paranoia might very well fail to re-supervene, not only because one no longer hears much news of car-jackings but also because the notion of anyone's having it in for me personally for any reason has come to seem not terribly plausible [although, I hasten to add, not quite "downright absurd"]; for as Dr. Johnson observes somewhere in his corpus of periodical essays, an apprehension of being the object of the malevolent designs of strangers is peculiarly characteristic of youth and with experience yields to the realization that people are for the most part utterly indifferent to one another, as it did in my case many years ago [although I cannot shake a more basal metaphysical paranoia that it would be just my stinkin' luck or fate for God or the devil suddenly to put it into some previously apathetic stranger's head to become my worst enemy {whence the immediately preceding square-bracketed parenthesis}]). I suppose all other things being equal, my parents would have been quite keen on my remaining fully automotive in Baltimore, but evidently their finances were then too tight to allow them to entertain the thought of purchasing a less choleric and sensitive replacement for the Aries, and it wasn't (at least as near as one could tell from a thousand miles' distance) as if I would actually need a car up there, seeing as how my apartment-to-be was sited right across the street from the university campus. And as for any prospective dependence on a car for sallies into remoter parts of the city for "extracurricular activities," that didn't even "factor in the equation," inasmuch as the necessity of covering all my costs via my even-then-far-from princely $9,000-per-annum stipend insured that such activities would be of rare if not non-existent occurrence. And then I suppose all three of us vaguely fancied that if extracurricular push came to shove, I could rely on public transportation to get me to my destination, simply because, as Baltimore was an ancient northern city, we assumed its public transportation was infinitely superior to Tampa's, although we (or at least I) knew that, seeing as how its subway had only one line and was supplemented rail-wise by an almost childishly primitive light-rail (whatever that was [if only the devisers or marketers of such light rail systems, which feebly sprouted up in dozens of mid-sized American cities in the eighties and nineties, had had the huevos to call them what they effectively were, namely, revivals of the old networks of streetcars, the entire public-transportation-scape of present-day urban America would doubtless now be a heck of a lot rosier, with nostalgia among the surviving riders of the original streetcars ensuring a goodly ridership from the beginning, and that ridership only swelling with the ever-crescent worldwide fetishization of the streetcar and cablecar-dominated San Francisco. But of course that would have required those hubristic technocrats to cast aside the pretense that these were systems of full-fledged trains and hence nearly as glamorous and expeditious as New York's subway, thLondon Underground, the Paris Metro, etc.), its public transportation consisted mainly of bus lines, and like every nice American of suburban or rural upbringing, I recoiled from the idea of taking a bus anywhere, my sole thitherto venture into urban bus travel, from the summer of 1988, when for a dozen or so consecutive weekday afternoons I rode one of Tampa's HART buses from the University of Tampa to my grandparents' house in West Tampa, then figuring in my memory like a metaphysically denuded "reboot" of Conrad's Marlowe's into the dark continent (or, rather, it would then have figured thereas had our metropolis's laughably picayune answer to Disney World, Busch Gardens, not styled itself the Dark Continent to distinguish itself as starkly as possible from its Oktoberfest-themed Virginian counterpart). And as it turned out, I managed largely to avoid using public transportation for the full four-year duration of my grad-school pseudo-career, as nine-tenths of all my colleagues' social gatherings occurred at the Charles Village Pub, sited only a block from my first and last apartments and still well within walking distance of the intervening three or four, and the smattering of residual student or faculty parties (the majority of which would fizzle out into a resolution to adjourn to the CVP anyhow) and outings to more remote watering holes such as the Club Charles in midtown, some two miles to the south of the campus, were transportationally dependent on carpooling or taxi-hailing--i.e., modes of getting to point B in which my pedestrianism did not figure as burdensome. But when in the summer of 1998 I dropped out of school by default (i.e., upon my funding running out and my receiving a call from the department's secretary announcing that I could remain in the program only by becoming a non-resident student and beginning to pay tuition, albeit only at a reduced rate, which being some then-unimaginably colossal sum like $3,000 dollars a year I could not possibly entertain paying [for I was not about to take out a loan for that all-too-dubious purpose, having already racked up $11,000 dollars in student-loan debt to supplement my stipend {i.e., essentially, to fund my beer and burger consumption at the CVP}]) without having formed the faintest ghost of a resolution to leave Baltimore, I had to find a job, and the swiftest apparent means of finding one was through a temp agency, and all the temp agencies, along with most of the businesses they supplied, were sited downtown, and the only affordable means of getting downtown in less than two hours was by bus. And so I very swiftly became a regular rider of the MTA buses that coursed up and down St. Paul and Charles Streets--the 3, the 11, and the 61. And I needs must say that while the first two years of my non-pseudo (if trivial) career as a downtown office worker were checkered at best, such that I was booted out of perhaps a double-dozen organizations before finally landing a temp position that turned permanent, I cannot blame any of its ignominious moments on the MTA--i.e., on the failure of that authority to provide me with sufficient means of getting to work on time. During morning rush hour, I seldom had to wait more than five minutes for a bus. And while these buses seldom attained speeds greater than that of an octogenarian cyclist, not even the fastest Jaguar or Porsche that concurrently traversed the route to downtown could boast of a more impressive performance, because crawling, bumper-to-bumper traffic was something every commuter had to cope with as a daily matter of course, what with downtown Baltimore's street system being essentially a snarled network of off-ramps from Interstates 83, 95, and 695 (this last limited access-er being the so-called Baltimore Beltway, a hypotrophic twin of its more famous Washingtonian semi-cousin). And as even in the earliest phase of my office career I was able to cobble together an annual income more than double that of my grad-school years, for the first time in my life I found a portion, however meager, of that income genuinely disposable (albeit only in concert with the gradual accretion of a credit-card balance), such that I now had an excuse for venturing even further afield of my home ZIP-code than downtown itself, and consequently of exploiting the MTA's extra-commutive capabilities on weekends and work holidays--of occasionally taking the Number 3 all the way to Towson to forage that eminently respectable suburb's formidably sized mall for new clothes, or taking the Number 11 all the way to Canton, in southeast Baltimore, for a solitary dinner at one of that exquisitely Yuppie neighborhood's upper-mid-market eateries. In short, I soon found myself becoming something of a man about town despite my carlessness; and epiphenominally and concurrently, all my bourgeois aversion to bus-riding vanished. I began to feel genuinely at home in the MTA bus system and to fall in love with it after a certain fashion. As for the subway non-system, as its above-mentioned single line terminated at one end at the Johns Hopkins medical campus, which was sited in a bleak, retail-free neighborhood east of downtown, and at the other in Owings Mills, a northwestern suburb whose sole retail highlight was a mall seemingly smaller and more downmarket than the one in Towson, I found it neither useful nor convenient and consequently avoided it altogether. Semi-ditto for the light rail, which did at least feature a pair or trio of spurs, and did at least pass through the western edge of downtown, such that I very occasionally used it to get thence to midtown for an after-work cocktail or concert (for in virtue of still possessing my Hopkins ID, I was able to purchase ten-dollar "student rush" tickets to performances of the Baltimore Symphony Orchestra, which took and take place at the Meyerhoff, a hall sited right next to the light rail's Mt. Royal stop), or to see off a visitor at BWI airport, the endpoint of one of the aforementioned spurs, or even to ride it northward all the way to Hunt Valley, a semi-rural suburb and site of a semi-stillborn and moribund mall from whose rear parking lot one could see the hills of southern Pennsylvania. But basically I was a dedicated and enthusiastic bus rider. But the reader who knows at least a thing-and-a-half about Baltimore is doubtless feeling that there is something a bit too pat and Pollyana about this description of my bus-riderhood, that there is something that I have deliberately left out of this description for fear of touching a third rail whose geographical reach is far more extensive than that of the Baltimore subway--namely, that of crime and public safety and its extricable implication in the perennially scabrous topic of interracial disharmony. Such a reader is doubtless wondering how despite having arrived in Baltimore as a wet-behind-the ears white lad (for although I have not yet divulged an explicit word about my so-called ethnic identity in the present essay, let us not guy ourselves: even if you know or knew nothing about me but my forename and surname and have not or had not read a word of my other writings, you have not or would not have interpellated me as any sort of American other than a white one [even if I am entitled, laughable as this sounds even to my own ears, to style myself Hispanic or Latino on account of my 25%-Cuban ethnic heritage]) who had thitherto lived entirely in crime-light suburban (or at best or worst, merely technically urban [Seriously, Volker: can you contemplate the notion of a hard-boiled detective novel or TV cop drama entitled The Mean Streets of Sarasota {not that even Sarasota is or was without its wrong side of the tracks, in this case the neighborhood known as New Town, whose unofficial eastern border was (and for all I know still is) a certain Winn Dixie supermarket that I knew better than to shop at, prudently confining my food-buying custom to the Publix directly across Tamiami Trail therefrom} without "busting a gut" or doing a so-called spit take if you happen to be drinking something at this moment?]) Florida, I ever came to feel at home in any portion of the transit system of a city notorious for a homicide rate higher than that of its most dangerous central-American counterparts and celebrated for its majority-black-cum-minority white demographics. But in all candor and frankness, I can assure such a reader that I have not applied so much as a square micrometer of whitewash to my recollection of my experience as a Baltimore mass-transit rider. Does this mean that such a reader is entitled syllogistically to infer that according to my lights the reportage on Baltimore as a crime-ridden Scheissloch always simmering and not-infrequently boiling with racial tension is grotesquely exaggerated? By no means. For I was already familiar with that reportage when my prospective move to Baltimore was but a twinkle in my callow Floridian undergraduate's eye, and I began to alight upon signs of the reportage's being well-founded within days of my arrival, and vis-à-vis my experience as a resident of Baltimore insgesamt and tout court my sense of Baltimore's being a town in which (to quote and extend the Clash's Joe Strummer's famous aperçu about Jamaica) "every white face is an invitation to robbery or some even more flagitious crime" only grew with each passing year. At my very first student party, I was given a talk about exactly how far and no further it was not imprudent to walk eastward of the campus at night, the talk-giver being a fellow-white liberal of a fourth or fifth-year grad who could not forbear ruefully perorating, "Your perspective on the whole racial situation changes pretty radically once you've been mugged at gunpoint." And even before that, within at most thirty-six hours of my arrival, during my first venture into the block of St. Paul street immediately behind my apartment, I found myself accosted in broad daylight if not by a full-blown mugger than by a pursuer of mugging by only slightly less belligerent means (cf. Clausewitz on politics and war, natch). The accoster was a thirtyish black man of slight build who claimed that he was stranded penniless in Baltimore, having traveled thither from some fairly remote Maryland suburb like Laurel or Bowie, and that he was hoping to get back home via "something called a MARC train" (i.e., one of the two MTA-administered commuter rail trains that ran between Baltimore and Washington on weekdays [and that I first heard of by name in this allusion of his to them]). My susceptibility to his spiel was doubtless augmented by his manner of speaking, which had nothing of the street or ghetto about it: it was fully rhotic and grammatical. He gave the impression of being a thoroughly respectable and sober middle-class gay man, although in hindsight his "twitchiness" throughout the delivery of his pitch suggests that he was "hopped up" on amphetamines or cocaine--the purchase of a fresh batch of which was doubtless the actual motive and goal of his cash-seeking antics. His "suggested contribution" was twenty dollars--four times as much as a one-way MARC ticket (as I of course did not know, but by the same superway token he could not have known that I did not know it [although by the verso of that selfsame token, he may very well have been within his epistemic rights to presume my ignorance, as the university's student body presumably did not teem with regular MARC-riders {and he was certainly within his epistemic rights to assume that a smooth-faced, tidily attired white lad such as my then self was a student}])--and to my everlasting shame I handed over to him a to-the-best-of-my-knowledge non-counterfeit Jackson banknote. To be sure that there "to my everlasting shame" merits some qualification, for my thitherto extremely negligible record of personal experience with street mendicants had given me no reason not to give him the so-called benefit of the doubt in full, no reason to be more than lightly inclined to suppose that his plight was feigned rather than genuine. For all that, that there record was not quite so close to nonexistent as to deprive me of other criteria for deciding whether or not to yield to his supplication--notably the criterion of ethical self-love, otherwise known as the innermost circle or top dog of the ordo amoris. I had been introduced to this criterion at some point very early in my second decade at the latest as a passenger in my father's truck or van, when he had stopped for gasoline at Fox's Corner, an ancient (est. ca. 1940) retail compound (I dare not insult it call it a "strip mall" because it started out as a single giant feed store and was divided into separate businesses only several decades after its foundation) about three miles down the road from our house. On his way back the van or truck from paying for the gas, he was accosted by a scruffy-looking thirty-something-year-old white dude sitting next to the front door, to which I was sitting close enough to hear that the man claimed that he was hungry and would appreciate some money for some food. My father brusquely rebuffed him and proceeded on his way. Whether I asked my father why he had not given the man any money or he simply volunteered the explanation I can no longer remember, but I vividly remember the explanation presumably nearly verbatim: he said that if he had given the man the money he would have been unable to feed my brother and me. This was doubtless an exaggeration, but I don't doubt that the five or ten or twenty dollars the man had been hoping for was or were not mere pocket change to my father; I don't doubt that if he had furnished him with that sum he would within the ensuing week have found himself five or ten or twenty dollars too poor to purchase some more or less essential good or service, some staple article of our household or his personal routine, and cursing himself for having been so cussedly soft-hearted at Fox's Corner a few days earlier. And twenty dollars was certainly money that I could not afford to squander on unvetted charity on that late August 1994 day when I did not even yet have a local bank account. Doubtless I would have been spared the inner ignominy of having flouted the ordo amoris had my father been present at my rencounter with that phony MARC-catcher; doubtless he would have told the rogue off as unceremoniously as he had told off his vulpine lair-squatting Floridian predecessor. And the tantalizing fact is that my father was still in Baltimore at the moment of that rencounter, and I cannot for the life of me recall why I was then on my own. Perhaps he, my father, was then having a nap in the ballroom-sized living room of my newly moved-into apartment only a few hundred feet behind me, or driving our rented moving van northward in order to drop it off at Budget's nearest branch in Towson, or, having already returned the van, walking along some stretch of the eight-mile distance from Towson to Charles Village, as he opted to do rather than sitting around waiting for a cab (and then remitting the astronomical twenty-dollar cabfare [yes, yes, yes: the same sum that I had thoughtlessly squandered on the phony MARC-hopper. Oh, the karma! One would need a tandoori chicken-scimitar to bisect the irony of it]). At the time I regarded this walk as a more than figuratively Herculean achievement; only four or five years later I came to find it somewhat less impressive when I was obliged to walk more or less the same walk when a friend in whose car I was a passenger had his vehicle temporarily expropriated from him by a Baltimore County police officer on account of its having expired license plates; and now that I manage to walk four miles a day despite being eight years older than my father was then, it has come to seem downright humdrumly achievable by the average male non-demigod. In any case, my father's assistance in forestalling future violations of the ordo amoris proved unnecessary, for less than a week later I beheld the very same rogue delivering the very same pitch to a fresh batch of junkie-bait. I would never "git fooled agin" by any of those sob story-peddlers, and indeed in the ensuing years I would become something of an expert at fending off their advances, at communicating my hard-heartedness with sufficient eloquence that they gave up on me almost as soon as they had started. I recall with particular vividness a certain fraudster of my third or fourth Baltimorean year, a fellow who claimed he had been obliged to separate himself from his starving children to go a-begging for them; he said that they were billeted in a certain suburb, adding with genuinely admirable thespian resourcefulness, "I don't know if you've heard of it." The second time I ran into him he got as far in his script as that expression of doubt (he evidently having failed to recognize me as one of his established prospective victims) before twigging my indifference; the third time he simply looked away from me with a mixture of sorrow, anger, and disgust when I was still a good fifty feet down-sidewalk of him. And as already indicated, within the first few days of my first academic term I was being warned about avoiding certain streets lest I should be mugged at gunpoint, and by the end of that selfsame term I had acquired a mental personal-safety map of the entire city that was to stand me in good stead for the next twenty years. By then I had learned that one would do well not to venture as far east of the campus as Greenmount Avenue, a scant six blocks in that direction,even in broad daylight; that the all-white "working-class" neighborhood of Hampden, which extended a good twenty blocks west of the campus, was more or less perfectly safe (albeit, per a stricture of a deceptively fine-boned and diminutive but perpetually sour-faced lily-white lesbian in my department, only in consequence of its organic "racism"). If one hoofed it due north as indicated by the weathercock atop the campus's central building, Gilman Hall, one could count on being unmolested all the way to the Mason-Dixon line, as that vector bisected the city's two poshest neighborhoods, Roland Park and Guilford before skirting the western (and posher) side of Towson and proceeding thence into the heart of Maryland's Horse Country, prevailingly inhabited by would-be and actual country squires residing in mammoth McMansions and ancient manor houses on double and triple-digit-acre estates. But one had only to walk a mile or so north-northeast of Gilman to find oneself in Waverley, a decidedly "sketchy" neighborhood set off from Guilford by Greenmount Avenue (which switched names to York Road [so called on account of being the old Colonial road leading that un-new Pennsylvanian town] right about there), and if you thence walked north you would not really be out of the crime-infested woods until you reached the traffic circle in downtown Towson. As for north-northeast's antipode, south-southwest, you could scarcely get your toes wet before you found yourself in Remington, superficially an extension of Hampden but far more dangerous, as I discovered when I tried living there beginning in my second year, in a dormer-windowed rowhouse-rooftop apartment. In vain did I once try ordering pizza from Angelo's, Hampden's foremost pizzeria, legendary for its "world's biggest slice", and sited only eight blocks away, at 36th and Keswick. The stroppy presumptively middle-aged organic white female Baltimorean down the line didn't even try to pretend that my address was beyond their delivery range, bluntly stating, "That's in Remington. We don't deliver there." And due westward of Remington lay Druid Hill Park, gateway to Baltimore's West Side (aptly so designated because like its Manhattan namesake it extended all the way to the city's western border), thirty solid square miles of top-shelf urban blight in which every white pedestrian was interpellated by default as a smack or crack-purchaser, as he was in the only slightly less aptly designated (because nibbled into towards the eastern city limit by Highlandtown, a sort of southeastern counterpart of Hampden that was the gateway to the southeastern suburb of Dundalk, the foremost butt of Central Marylanders' Deliverance jokes) East Side. Due south of Homewood you were pretty much safe until about 25th Street, where walking after dark was slightly inadvisable (for by day one only had to cope with an admittedly numerous tribe of irascible panhandlers fresh from the loony bin), as it remained until Mt. Vernon Square, home of the original (and much prettier) Washington Monument, and thence it was smooth downhill sailing along and through so-called Restaurant Row, the central business district (which, although eerily deserted at night [cf. Jonathan Richman's "Lonely Financial Zone" mutato mutando {i.e., making allowances for its Bostonian setting}, natch] never felt at all dangerous even then, for over the years I complacently waited for many a nocturnal bus and taxi there), the Disneyworldesquely safe Inner Harbor, the highly gentrified and almost-posh neighborhood of Federal Hill, and finally Brooklyn, Baltimore's southernmost neighborhood and another fitting namesake of a New-York counterpart (or at least the Archie and Edith Bunker-friendly portions thereof), for while it was as white and "working class" as Dundalk and Hampden, its pallid proletarianism was interpellated as properly urban rather than as an alien excrescence of rural Appalachia. In short, from my first year as a Baltimorean onwards I believed (and believed with what I have no reason to believe was not good reason, especially with the benefit of all I have learned of so-called Charm City since) that I would have to confine my ambulatory exploration of the town to a half-mile wide band or spine running through it from north to south (although honorable mention must be given to two extra-spinal neighborhoods--Locust Point, which hugged the long stretch of street connecting the southern side of the Inner Harbor to the historical shrine of Fort McHenry and was, like Brooklyn, an altogether wholesome white working-class district, and the aforementioned Canton, which was [and is] sited immediately to the west of Highlandtown and was {and, as far as I know, still is} basically Federal Hill East). To have ventured a foot eastward or westward seemed in or to my eyes as perversely reckless as venturing into the ocean-portions cartographically labeled "Here be Dragons" would have seemed in or to the eyes--or, rather, eye [for the other one would naturally have been missing and its socket masked by a piratic eye-patch]] of a fifteenth-century sea captain. (Here of course a vile Whig will interject that my conceit is decidedly ill conceived, inasmuch as those very supposedly dragon-infested waters turned out to be the New World, but my withers are unwrung by this interjection, for I am of one mind with Dr. Johnson in conjecturing that the Old World and New alike would have ended up much better off had Columbus never undertaken his voyage.) (I own I did exactly once try to be a good little white liberal by aiming to do a day's non-grocery shopping at Mondawmin Mall, the only retail compound within the city limits large enough to be honestly styled a mall [for the Northern Hampden/Southern Roland Park-sited Rotunda Mall was essentially a Giant Supermarket with a handful of moribund niche boutiques attached to it]. But the "Waitin' for My Man"-esque "vibes" I received from its massed clientele within seconds of crossing its main entrance's threshold were sufficiently hostile to send me instantly scurrying back down into the subway [for I own I did take the subway just that one time]). And that belief was still firmly in place when I began my career as an MTA bus-rider three years later. And yet for at least the first fifteen years of that career I never felt unsafe on an MTA bus. Why? I suppose a prosaic but nontrivial portion of the reason was that the buses were amply furnished with security cameras. These were small enough to be held in one's hand and rendered somewhat less obtrusive by being encased in spheres of smoky sunshade-glass, but they were none too discreetly placed, such that one was always aware of being under their watchful artificial eyes--the eyes not of Big Brother but rather a sort of coolly benevolent cousin of HAL 9000. And so one assumed that any ruffian or scapegrace inclined by will or habit to mug or rough up a fellow-passenger would have long since come to curb that habit as a matter of course. But the happy truth and fact of the matter is or was that one very seldom got the impression that even the most louche or belligerent-looking of one's busmates was even ever so vaguely or faintly inclined to criminal activity. I am not about to pretend that the typical MTA bus ride was like one of those cinema-comedy package-tourism scenes (there must be hundreds of movies containing such scenes, but I regrettably can't remember any of them by name) wherein one particularly chipper bus passenger engages all the others to accompany him in a singalong. But I am honestly going to assert that it was more or less an automotive analogue to one of those dramaturgically neutral, pace-slackening movie scenes set in a public library; which is to say for the most part people minded their own business, and that to the extent that the exigencies of the journey required them (e.g., to make way for them on their way to either of the two exits, or to remove some article to make room for them on a seat) they were generally very polite, even when it was standing room only and there were dozens of us crammed in there like proverbial tinned sardines, or more nearly literally, like commuters "on a Japanese subway" (to quote a younker who loudly drew everyone's attention to this resemblance so often that I came privately to nickname him "Japanese Subway Guy"). And when disorder did occasionally supervene, it was of a decidedly innocent and unthreatening sort. I recall, for example, a return-commute moment when a tall, late-middle-aged light-skinned black fellow with long untidy hair climbed in with a large paper bag evidently containing a large bottle of liquor and began laying censoriously into the ridership en masse in a stentorian voice, and in sonorously rounded periods, like a prophet or preacher (it really was a declamation worthy of James Earl Jones and Roscoe Lee Browne in their most dignified roles), the only shortcoming of the performance from a sermon-connoisseur's point of hearing being its extremely high quotient of profanity, which eventually elicited a bit of firm if gentle "push-back" from a rider or two of comparable age and gravitas. Whereupon he thundered, "You're fucking damned right I'm cursing! Go to church on Sunday, and curse all day Monday!" But having discharged his fury in this culminating interjection (perhaps a deliberate "against-the-grain" allusion to the classic gospel song "I'm Going to Live the Life I Sing About in My Song"), he immediately took a seat and was silent thereafter. Of course, now that I have disclosed that this crapulous potty-mouthed preacher was black, a certain type of reader (I believe that the term of art for a person of this type is a "shitlib") has tricked himself into concluding that he has twigged the real or at least the main reason I felt perfectly safe on the MTA buses. I write that he will have tricked himself into his conclusion because he will have formed it in the very teeth of his knowledge that Baltimore is (and was) a majority poor-black city and his assumption that the ridership of municipal bus services is prevailingly composed of people too poor to buy a car. And of course his bad-faith conclusion is that I felt safe on Baltimore buses because within their confines I was prevailingly surrounded by other white people, and that I only interpellated the supervention of the preacher as a manifestation of disorder (however unthreatening) because he was black; that had he been an equally crapulous dead-ringer for Billy Graham or Jimmy Swaggart his supervention would never have carved itself into my memory as a noteworthy event. But of course this conclusion is entirely erroneous, and the fact was and is that racially speaking the ridership of the MTA buses mapped perfectly onto the city's census demographics--i.e., that sixty to seventy-percent of my fellow-riders were black and only thirty-to-forty percent of them were white. And so to assert that I felt perfectly safe on MTA buses is to assert, inter alia, that I felt perfectly safe being a member of a white minority when riding them. And I then felt perfectly safe in my whiteness simply because I did not then feel myself assailed by the waves of black animosity I had encountered or would encounter at Mondawmin Mall. And yet of course there would have been considerable overlap in any so-called Venn diagram conjointly presenting the set of black MTA riders and the set of black Mondawmin Mall shoppers; indeed, I daresay that Venn diagram would have come close to displaying a single perfect circle. I own that the discrepancy in attitude baffles me to this day, but at least for the moment we need not try to get to the bottom of it. For the moment it suffices to observe that however ardent a "racist" I may have been and may still be, my "racism" did not scare me off from riding the bus. I own that there was a single exception to the bus qua demographic scale model, namely the above-mentioned 61, on which whites outnumbered blacks perhaps ten to one, and I will own that when riding it I did occasionally feel a frisson of Gemütlichkeit on realizing that I was surrounded by "people who looked like me" for a change. Why, I even struck up a near handful of acquaintanceships with some of these fellow-"honkeys" on the 61. There was Jason F., an enterprising youngish futures trader with admirably cultivated taste in literature (why, he was even a fan of such obscure masterpieces as the Soviet-era Russian novelist Yuri Olesha's Envy and did not shrink from acquainting himself with such notoriously difficult modern-canonical American writers as William Gaddis [although he was failing to find himself becoming a fan of the latter on account of J.R.'s wildly unrealistic {to his mind} depiction of the workings of financial markets]) and music (he having attended live performances of the Lyric Opera of Chicago), not to mention a wickedly puckish sense of humor. Later on there was another Jason, Jason D., a scientist at the Maryland Department of the Environment who was likewise literarily cultivated. And I mustn't (and indeed can't) forget that grave pink-faced old gent who bore an uncanny resemblance to W.G. Sebald and always boarded in impeccable preppy beachwear (pastel polo shirts and khaki chino slacks) while carrying a mahogany leather briefcase, a semi-incongruity that led me to conjecture with perhaps a mite too great meta-vocational specificity (for I suppose he could just as easily have been a semi-retired investment banker at T.Rowe Price) that he was an emeritus professor at the University of Maryland's medical campus. Even so, I must admit that my memoryscape of the remainder of my frequented routes is not devoid of black near-analogues to these engaging figures. My favorite was a certain portly and good-natured fellow who always sat in a wheelchair sited in the space allocated to the disabled at the front of the bus. I supposed his wheelchair-boundess was owing not to any bone injury or neurological disorder but to some disease of excess, for at one point he mentioned that he suffered from (the) gout. He was extremely locquacious, filling every minute of his ride with an ever-chipper monologue addressed to his constant companion, a perennially silent woman of about his own age. (It really was like something out of a Thomas Bernhard play or novel, only one in which the protagonist-cum-monlogist is a happy, well-adjusted person rather than an incorrigible misanthrope.) On one occasion, when he noticed that I was reading King Lear, he withdrew his parole from his lady-friend long enough to report to me that a new televisiual or cinematic production of that play had recently been released, and that while he found the acting excellent, he had some reservations about the mise-en-scène (for it was one of those productions in which the historical time of reference has been updated to ten minutes ago, a dramaturgical practice of which he rightly stridently disapproved). And there was that other black fellow--or perhaps I should rather say gentleman: a fifty-something man in conspicuously understatedly dapper attire, which is to say not the flashy street-pimp-style semi-formalwear that one expects of the fashion-conscious urban black man of a certain age, but a sort of outfit that any mid-to-late twentieth century American man might have donned as a matter of course if he were properly dressing up on any non work-related occasion--say, a tweed sports jacket, dark blue vest, dark red necktie, navy blue slacks, and well-polished brown Oxford brogues. I saw this gentleman only once, on the Number 3 or 11, but during those ten or twenty minutes of our co-ridership he revealed himself to be very much a man after my own heart, at least in the sartorial-cum-haberdasherial department, not only via his clothing but also via his discourse, for throughout the ride he reminsced aloud to his banquette-mate (evidently a boyhood friend) about the sartorial-cum-haberdasherial glories of the Baltimore department stores of old, and lamented the near-total absence of such glories from the chain stores that had since succeeded them. And yet, he reported, amid all this desolation he had recently alighted upon a single pair of Stacy Adams-brand dress shoes and had snatched them up like a shot, for Stacy Adams had been his favorite shoe-brand in his adolescent years. And his dropping of that name Stacy Adams was like toasted manna to my heart-cockles, for although I couldn't say (or couldn't have said) that Stacy Adams had ever been my favorite shoe brand, I could have said that I myself had purchased or been given a pair of Stacy Adams dress shoes not so very long before, perhaps as recently as the early oughties, that at that time my parents had assured me that Stacy Adams was a top-flight shoe brand, perhaps that it had even been my father's shoe-brand during his mid-1960s adolescence, when he had been almost preppyish in his fashion choices, that I had thought those shoes mighty fine-looking and that they had done me yeoman's service at least until their soles had worn out (at which time I had presumably simply discarded them, as I have regrettably been obliged to discard all my dress shoes since the virtual extinction of mass-retail resoleable footwear and the non-boutique cobbling trade in the late 1990s). And by the same token (!) I recall certain 61-set moments in which I found certain of my fellow-Caucasians downright insufferable. The most insufferable of all of these was a certain fortyish woman with jawline-length lightly graying dark brown hair whose voice was absolutely indistinguishable from that of a certain soccer (or, rather, little-league baseball) mom in a laundry-detergent commercial from the 1980s who had rounded out the ad by staring the camera sour-pussedly in the eye and uttering the phrase "especially the dipose-all" with insufferable smugness and superciliousness in that most irritating of Midwestern accents, the Wisconsinian-Minnesonian (I like to call it the cheesehead accent) one, dispose-all being her preferred term of abuse for the most gluttonous (and hence most aggressively cloth-staining) of her eight (or whatever number a full little-league baseball team consists of) sons. But it was not merely her voice that recalled the woman from the commercial: she seemed in a manner of speaking to be a sort of spiritual or moral cousin of her, for I recall her once reporting to my horror (but for the ears of her preferred bus-interlocutor, a woman of more advanced years) that she and her husband were raising their own sole tyke of a son to urinate exclusively while sitting down. So she was a shitlib of perhaps the very worst stripe--a so-called ball-busting feminist whose greatest pleasure is to put males of all ages in the most humiliatingly emasculating positions. (Here the reader may believe he has caught me out in a self-contradiction inasmuch as many if not most soccer moms have not been ball-busting feminists, but in truth I do not contradict myself inasmuch as ball-busting feminism is but a sort of ultracrepidarian aggravation of the worst vices of mothers of all political persuasions.)

At this point I was about to apologize for the apparent gratuitousness and digressiveness of the above mini-memoir of my years as a Baltimore bus-rider and go on to explain that it was not gratuitous or digressive at all but super-germane to the aim and purpose of the present essay, but then it suddenly dawned on me that I had not yet explained what that aim and that purpose were or or even made plain that the present essay had any aim or purpose at all beyond serving as a full-scale memoir of my lifetime automobilophobia. For the reader has doubtless interpellated the present essay as just such a memoir, and who can blame him if he has? For after all, the memoir of this type has become a genre unto itself, a sob story-cum-autoeulogy whose interest for the reader consists (or aims to consist) in allowing him to admire the author-cum-subject as an exquisitely eccentric oddball, a latter-day "irregular humorist," while pitying him as a perennially square peg in a round hole; in allowing him simultaneously to exclaim "O brave new world that has such people in it!" and "there but for the grace of God (or "my genes" or "my happy childhood," etc.) go I." Of course (or, rather no course whatsoever), by "the reader" I here mean a reader for whom the present essay is the first "original" piece of writing by me he has ever encountered, or perhaps even a reader who has already read some substantial fraction of my essayistic output, but certainly not my ideal reader, the reader who has carefully read and faultlessly remembered everything I have ever written and made public, for that reader would know that I would be highly unlikely to pen a memoir of the type in point inasmuch as one of my essays, "Perpendicular Work," is a so-called hit piece on a memoir of that type, Tim Page's Parallel Play, wherein the author documents his "struggles" (i.e., triumphs masquerading as struggles) as a "sufferer" (i.e., savorer masquerading as a suffere) from "Asperger's Syndrome" (i.e., run-of-the-mill masculine equability futilely seeking refuge from hysterical feminine sentimentalism behind a screen of psychobabble). But the best example and locus classicus of such an essay--at least to the extent that it best exhibits everything I find wrong-headed and reprehensible about the genre--is Wish I Could Be There: Notes from a Phobic Life by Allen Shawn, younger brother of the famous actor Wallace Shawn. Not that I have ever read the accursed book, but I have heard an interview with the author on NPR's Fresh Air (also the source of the interview that apprised me of Page's book [as my ideal reader will recall]) that told me everything I needed to know about it. Indeed, the portion of its title to the right of its colon says that everything in little. You see, per the interview, Shawn reckoned he found life much harder to slog through than normal people did because unlike them he was painfully afraid of the acts and experiences it regularly required one to perform and undergo. Whenever, for example, he had to take an airplane flight to visit a friend or relative, he would find himself worrying to the point of sleeplessness about the possibility of the plane's crashing (and his dying a horrible death in the crash, natch). Mind you, he always dutifully boarded the plane anyway, but it was the phobic thought that counted, wasn't it? No, it jolly well wasn't, at least according to my lights, for I reckoned that it was not only perfectly rational but perfectly sensible to be scared sleepless during the run-up to an airplane journey. Sure, I was familiar with all the statistical evidence cited in supposed watertight refutation of any objections to airline travel--notably with the proverbial argument that you were more likely to die from slipping in your bathtub than from taking a plane ride; but by my reckoning that evidence had no bearing whatsoever on whether or not one ought to be afraid of flying. For it seemed to me that the statistical likelihood of the materialization of a given threat to one's life had to be weighed against the prospective phenomenal appallingness of experiencing that materialization, and that when that appallingness exceeded the threshold of comprehensibility, one was well within one's rational rights to be afraid of it unless the likelihood was more than figuratively zero. I daresay being devoured alive by a man trap-sized Venus fly trap would be more appalling than dying in a plane crash, but because man trap-sized Venus fly traps don't exist, it is silly or insane to be afraid of dying by being devoured alive by one. On the other hand, dying in an airplane crash is a distinct possibility, however remote, because airplanes do exist and have been known to crash, however rarely. And I reckon that however ardent an air-travel enthusiast the reader may be, he, like me, will be hard-pressed to think of a more appalling way of dying than in an airliner crash, or to take the full measure of its appallingness. And inasmuch as it is entirely sensible to fear dying an incomprehensibly appalling death, it is entirely sensible to fear traveling by plane. Moreover (to fast-backward to 2007 qua year of publication of Shawn's book per the online reference source of first resort, and therefore the presumptive year of my audition of the above-mentioned interview]), as I knew from the half-dozen flights I had taken (the most recent of these having occurred in July of 1995, when I had flown from Baltimore Washington-International Airport to Tampa International Airport for my maternal grandfather's funeral [not that the funeral was held at the airport!], after which I had always traveled to and from Tampa and Baltimore via Amtrak train [although oddly enough, I recall the return flight to Baltimore in '95 as the least anxiety-ridden of all butcher's half-dozen flights of my adulthood, largely owing to my having nearly the entire coach section of a 747 to myself owing to a post-last-minute rebooking owing to my having arrived at the terminal a few minutes after the departure of my originally scheduled flight {pace the discursive upshot of the next parenthesis, I dare say one would not find any airline so obliging towards a flight-misser nowadays}]) the experience of air travel (setting aside all the misery attending the pre-boarding and post-touchdown bureaucratic rigmaroles, about which I am obviously too superannuated a flyer to comment on with any authority [although of course I have heard that they have become far more degrading and time-consuming than it used to be]) is ineluctably attended by all sorts of phenomena that by default exert the somatic effect of impending death even if they rarely if ever terminate in a mortality. First and least fatally, throughout the journey one's ears are assailed by the roar of the engine. Of course the ergonomics experts have done their best to muffle it, but it is always there, droning none too discreetly away, reminding one that one is but inches away from being as efficiently turned into toasted mincemeat as the latest hapless seagull or albatross to have crossed flight paths with the craft. Then there are of course the inevitable spells of turbulence; to be sure, most of them merely cause the plane momentarily to jolt a few vertical or feet, but a substantial minority cause the flight attendants to lose their balance, sometimes even to the point of involuntarily sending their tray of refreshments tumbling onto the aisle (where they would shatter into potentially lethal shards and splinters of glass in any civilized terrestrial hospitality-setting, but of course the powers that be have forestalled all such vitrine mishaps by having all the cups and dishes made of paper or plastic [a provision which, like the omission of knives from the cutlery of prison inmates, serves but as a grim reminder of the de-facto murderousness of the forces in play]). Then there are the awful dozen or so minutes after takeoff when the plane is climbing to its destined cruising height: all the while, the plane is cocked at a reclining angle, an angle of such acuity that if it were shared with one's present seat (to say nothing of one's living-room "Easy Boy" recliner back [and down] home), one would expect to fall backwards onto one's head (and into the lap of the passenger behind one [which body part would admittedly cushion the concussion, but en revanche one knows better than to trust a person suddenly "gifted" with a lapful of human head not to react violently to the bestowal]); and all the while the air is getting perceptibly thinner in ruthless defiance of the internal climate-control system, its attenuation attested to by the popping of one's ears--a miniature prelude of the full-body explosion if the ascent continued unchecked for but a few more minutes and into the realm of outer space, "where nobody can hear you scream." And don't get me started on the shocks visited on the human organism by the descent, when every few seconds one experiences the very same stomach-uplifting sensation one feels on one of those fairground rides that simulate the fall of an unreliable parachute, and that one would presumably experience in a skyscraper elevator whose suspending cable had just been severed. "Don't get me wrong": I realize, or at least have been led to believe by "credible sources," that one gets used to all these meta-somatic anomalies after the first double-dozen or so flights; and I am of course aware that a substantial minority if not outright majority of experienced flyers even affect to derive a certain frisson of exhilaration from them, but the fact that such inurement occurs en masse can do nothing to detract from the intrinsic ominousness of the phenomena, or from the unnaturalness and barbarity of the subjective-cum-organic tolerance built up by repeated exposure to them. In short, the fear of flying is an entirely natural and rational fear--indeed, it is so entirely natural and rational that I dare say no human being has ever succeeded at fully conquering it. Why, the selfsame people (i.e., more or less every other living soul one has ever met) who are constantly evangelizing against us uncloseted aviophobes betray their own unnaturally suppressed aviophobia in the very phraseology of their sermons: "You can't just live your life hiding under your bed," they all say (usually either immediately before or after citing the statistic about the bathtub), or "You can't just stay stuck in one place your whole life"--all words to the ungainsayable effect of "Yes, this is something legitimately to be scared of, but the present system of life doesn't allow any decent person to avoid dealing with it." And to these words I have ready to hand what I believe to be two retorts or objections of watertight irrefutability. The first is that a certain global public-health event of four to six years ago conclusively proved that more or less everyone was capable of living more or less under his bed for a substantial portion of his life, and even more materially, that a good chunk of the Occidental population ("ironically" a chunk whose Venn-diagram overlap with the chunk thereof composed of anti-aviophobes would be quite extensive albeit not quite coextensive) were quite content to continue living that way indefinitely--and this in response to a threat that was "arguably" less mortally ominous than air travel. (And as I have put scare quotes around "arguably" I am duty-bound qua uncloseted non-argumentophobe to show that I am not too scared to tender the argument along with its qualifying counterargument in one handy-dandy sentence thus: Yes, even if one tends to believe, as I do, that the COVID 19 virus was and is {for we mustn't forget that it's still out there, that eight years on one occasionally still sees headlines warning of a new mutation of the undead animalcule!} no more intrinsically pernicious than that of the common cold, one must acknowledge that one definitely runs a much higher risk of being carried off by a common- cold infection (or, to be more precise, by complications arising thereform) than of dying in an airplane crash, but en revanche one must acknowledge that in point of appallingness the typical death resulting from a common-cold infection lies at the very antipode of the typical death by aircrash--that it is, in fact, a death of the most eminently imaginable sort, a death wherein one's final moments are spent in a comfortably recumbent posture, and in a semi-conscious pharmacalogically palliated state while surrounded by one's so-called loved ones, etc.). The second is that it is disingenuous to assert that shunning air travel marks a retreat from the current system of life as radical as never leaving one's dwelling-space; for it in fact merely exacts the forgoing of certain amenities of that system that are "in the final analysis" either peripheral or trivial. So one will never see the stinkin' Great Pyramids in the stony flesh. Fine: the interweb is a repository of approximately a zillion free photographs of each and every one of the Great Pyramids snapped from every conceivable angle and time of day and year (not to mention, presumably [for I have never thought to investigate the matter {although it has just now occurred to me that being as I am an ardent fan of Samuel Johnson's "oriental" novella Rasselas, in whose plot a visit to the interiors of the Great Pyramids figures rather prominently, I might agreeably while away many an hour in such an investigation}] another zillion of each and every nook and cranny of each and every burial chamber therein]). So one will never again see the friends of one's youth who have moved to other parts of the country, or to other countries entirely. Fine: one can stay in touch with them remotely, which in this day and age should be easy enough to do no matter which corner of the globe they have absconded to, given that way back in the 1750s, when of course the only medium of remote communication was the paper letter and paper letters were of course still conveyed exclusively by horse-drawn carriages and sailing ships, Samuel Johnson could confidently write (in the just-parenthetically-mentioned Rasselas, via his transparent surrogate-cum-mouthpiece, the poet-cum-sage Imlac) that throughout then-contemporary Europe "[t]here is such communication between distant places that one friend can hardly be said to be absent from another." In short and in fine, I reckon that if he had his druthers, no sensible present-day living person would ever strap himself into an airplane-seat, and that his druthers are much more easily obtainable (or attainable?) than he supposes. Of course, at first blush, that there "he supposes" may seem to be fairly oozing with patrician contempt for my fellow present-day Occidentals' "false consciousness" or "lack of self-awareness," but the virtue whose faultiness or absence I am ascribing to them is in fact one for which anyone may readily be forgiven for lacking in ideal abundance, inasmuch as it is possessed in such abundance by God alone. It is a virtue or attribute most capaciously encapsulated by the German noun Besonnenheit, which is usually translated (by, inter alia, the present writer in his Englishings from that language) as "presence of mind," a rendering that still seems to me the best one that doesn't greatly exceed the original in typographical extent; but it misleadingly suggests that the exercise of this virtue, Besonnenheit, is confined to a certain genre of mental moment, a moment when one has to make some sort of split-second life-and-death decision like whether or not to jump out of a flaming crashing airplane, whereas it is in fact most fully exercised in a certain other genre thereof--a moment, or, rather, time-unit of generally lingering length, when one is surveying some vast immobile object at leisure and looking out for flaws and discrepancies in its facture and judiciously correcting and reconciling them as one finds them. To my mind no writer has yet done a better job of conveying what Besonnenheit is all about than E.T.A. Hoffmann (here I would have added "of Tales of Hoffmann fame" had I been able to kid myself into believing that either Hoffmann's tales or the opera based on them were still famous by the most latitudinarian measure), specifically in his essay on Ludwig van Beethoven's instrumental music, and even more specifically in the section therein devoted to that composer's Fifth Symphony. Here, he addresses the manifold resourceful uses to which Beethoven puts the work's famous opening four-note theme (I hope I'm not kidding myself in supposing that the opening four notes of the Fifth are still famous in the most bigoted sense), emphasizing not only its myriad meaning-impregnated iterations and transpositions in the first movement but also its no less meaning-impregnated disguised return in the third-movement run-up to the finale, wherein it takes on an entirely new significance in virtue of the long stretch of pastoral equability that has preceded it and the triumph that is to follow it, and also in virtue of being played slowly and in triple time; the overall effect, per Hoffmann, being that of colossal cosmic forces that will inexorably crush us all completely if...well...I suppose if we don't get our Besonnenheit together like Beethoven. Now, the finished product of the complete symphony gives us the impression that Beethoven comprehended in a flash exactly which notes conveyed which meanings in relation to each other, but we (meaning myself and the perhaps ten other still-living people who have seen Leonard Bernstein's brilliant piano keyboard-aided demonstration, on one of his CBS Omnibus broadcasts, of Beethoven's revision of the first movement) now know that it actually took Beethoven quite a long time to work out what to do with that four-note theme and what it was capable of signifying, that he blackened dozens of pages of score-paper with augmentations, iterations, harmonizations, and variations of that theme that sound downright bizarre to us now that we know what he eventually managed to do with it; such that his Besonnenheit only very slowly came into its own. And I submit that the aviophobia-sceptical reader must submit his de facto chaotic and nebulous collection of memories of his plane trips to exactly the same sort of meticulous and exhaustive treatment, that he must examine each of these memories as best he can in relation to each other and to his entire recollected affective landscape, his entire inwardly recorded history of agreeable and disagreeable experience, whereupon I wager that he will at length discover the true affective heft and flavor of each of those flights. He will discover, for instance, that that flight from Los Angeles to Kalamazoo that he has likened (in a kind of private legend that has become a public nuisance among his acquaintances thanks to his repetition of it in anecdote after anecdote) to a five-hour nap on an eiderdown bed was only comparatively tranquil, that it has only come to seem the ne plus ultra of tranquility because it was the first and last flight in ages in which he wasn't obliged to reach for the airsickness bag, or in which the pilot didn't announce with palpably forced chipperness that owing to "cloud cover" or "inclement weather" the plane was "still awaiting clearance to land." And he will discover that many of those places he has squandered so many thousands of frequent-flier miles on getting to weren't all they had been cracked up to be, that on reflecting on his experience of the Great Pyramids or the Louvre or the Great Wall of China he feels very much like the young Texan in the film who on returning from Europe remarks that he still feels as if he has "only seen it on TV." And after that besonnenheitsvoll moment--or, rather, long succession of moments--he will begin to have second thoughts about booking that first-ever trip to Sydney or Machu Pichu. And naturally I believe that mutatis mutandis the same may be said of every sensible living person's basal and essential attitude to driving and to the lifestyle modifications that would inexorably flow from a fully besonnenheitsvoll exploration of that attitude. I will not deny that the mutandi associated with both the aversion to driving and the "cost-benefit analysis" of the renunciation of driving are many and significant; I will not pretend that driving is as disagreeable and frightening in exactly the same way as flying is, or that the sacrifices one typically has to make in giving up driving are the same as those one typically has to make in giving up flying; nay, I will not deny that these sacrifices are far more numerous and far less practicable in the present system of life. Nonetheless, I believe that a goodly chunk of sensible Occidentals will on giving due consideration to their attitude to driving and to the trade-offs entailed by not driving will conclude that these trade-offs are ultimately worth making. Hence (here finally comes the explanation!) my mini-memoir about my years as a Baltimore bus-rider, which I have tendered by way of putting myself on a more nearly even besonnenheitig footing with the reader, of showing him that once upon a time I was not nearly as besonnenheitig about my aversion to driving as I am now, that indeed, once upon a time I was as benighted vis-à-vis certain sectors of my aversionscape of driving as he presumably is vis-à-vis the corresponding sectors of his. Before becoming a seasoned Baltimore bus-rider I knew that I hated driving, but I was either oblivious of or imperfectly acquainted with all the reasons I hated it, and I was likewise but ill-acquainted with the affective obstacles that stood in the way of my becoming a committed non-driver. Theretofore I was conscious of but a fraction (albeit a fairly large fraction) of the amenities that I had been forgoing when putting myself behind the wheel of a moving automotive vehicle. Before becoming a seasoned Baltimore bus-rider I thought it might be worthwhile to resume driving despite my intense aversion to it for the sake of avoiding the shady and ethnically alien characters that typically rode buses; ever since then I have been firmly committed to not driving both because I have found that I can bear the casual company of such characters equably enough and because I have discovered certain amenities associated with bus-riding that I was forgoing unbeknownst to myself back in my days as a driver. I shall enumerate and describe these conveniences in dew coarse (i.e., most likely, several dozen pages hence); but at the moment it beseems me to enumerate the disamenities that initially made me averse to driving because to this day they remain the principal ones in my meta-automotive aversionscape and I believe they are those that are most intrinsically dissuasive, those that any sensible person ought to be as intensely averse to as I am. As with aviophobia, the first among these is the type of death one imagines the activity in question resulting in. In this case there are actually two such types: the first consisting of a (say) three to ten-second interval of blind panic after which "all is darkness for ever" and the second of a (say) three-to-ten-week interval of excruciating pain exacerbated by the awareness that one has lost a limb or two (or three or four), that even if one survives one will never walk, feed oneself, play the violin, autc., again. I suppose most people would argue that at least the first of these two sub-types is preferable to death by plane crash, as it doesn't leave one enough time to become fully conscious of the fate one is about to meet and because the panic is not infrequently alloyed with a dose of hope as one does one's best to swerve out of the way of the oncoming wall, "Mac Truck," autc. But by the same token, one must acknowledge that the extra time imposed by the death by plane crash in addition to making one conscious of that fate affords one the opportunity to prepare oneself for meeting it, to make peace with one's maker, and perhaps even valedictorially tell one's spouse or offspring that one loves him, her, or them, either in person if they are fellow-passengers or (onboard WI-FI-security and service permitting) via mobile phone if they are not. And of course the second type of the auto-automotively occasioned death characteristically affords such a valedictory avowal, albeit (in some versions of the scenario) only in the halting muddle-mouthed cadences of brain damage-occasioned aphasia. But no matter how you slice it (or yourself), death by automobile-crash is one of the more horrible types of death. It is also one that one is much more likely to experience than death by airplane crash. How much more likely? Oh, I would say about a zillion times more. Well, I suppose they aren't quite that high, but they might as well be, for qua epistemological touchstone vis-à-vis any sort of event statistics always take a very distant second place to the question whether or not the sort of event in question has ever happened to someone one has known--not necessarily known personally in the sense of having met the person, mind you; for anyone in the outermost precincts of one's social circle will do; anyone a friend or family member has mentioned as a friend or family member of a friend, etc. As soon something has happened to someone one knows even in this broadest sense, one immediately regards it as something that may very easily happen to oneself. I feel a certain nagging obligation to humor the citers of statistics on air-crash deaths because I have never known anyone even in this broadest sense, but I feel not even the most quiescent obligation to humor the citers of statistics on automobile-accident deaths because I have known at least seven people who have died in car accidents. Three of these seven were even known to me personally in the strictest sense; in both chronological order and ascending order of intimacy of acquaintance they are a friend and coworker of my father, who died behind the wheel of his pick-up truck in the early 1980s; a young man who sat immediately in front of me in my sophomore-year math class and with whom I exchanged a fair amount of banter over the course of those eight or nine months (for his accident occurred at the very end of the school year, on his drunken way home from a graduation party at the house of another fellow I knew, a near neighbor of ours [both lads being just-graduated seniors]); and my paternal grandfather, who perished instantly in April of 1994 when his tiny pickup truck crashed into the rear of a large transport vehicle in Alabama during the return drive from his annual visit to his brothers up in Iowa. (As there were no roadside witnesses, to this day nobody knows what caused the crash; according to my father, the medical examiner thought that he had most likely had a massive stroke that instantly carried him off, such that he had already been dead at the moment of the collision [cf., interestingly if irrelevantly enough, the speculation after W.G. Sebald's death several years later in a comparably inexplicable car crash that a massive heart attack had knocked him unconscious while he was behind the wheel].) When one has known so many people who have died as an immediate consequence of engaging in a certain activity, surely one cannot even begin to dream of engaging in that activity oneself. Well, of course one surely can not only dream of engaging in it but instantly resume engaging in it as a matter of course, for I learned of my grandfather's death from my parents at the beginning of a visit by them to my college-lodgings in Sarasota, to which they had just driven from their house some sixty miles to the north-northwest thereof. Moreover, to the best of my recollection nobody gasped or gaped with incredulity at their embarking on such a dangerous road trip so soon after the automobile-actuated death of a loved one. And of course for work reasons my father had been blithely tooling all around the state in various automotive vehicles for all dozen years since his buddy's fatal truck crash. (I believe that in ca. 1986 he had even driven all the way to Washington, DC to take a lucrative and potentially permanent pipe-fitting job, only to drive all the way back to Tampa on concluding that he wasn't up uprooting us all from our native Floridian soil.) (Whether my fully [if ever-more-anxiously] automotive mother's meta-automotive necrology contains any obits absent from both mine and my father's is, I am afraid, a mystery to me. I'll have to ask her about it sometime.) And presumably these expressions of astonishment had been wanting because everybody in my parents' circle would have reflexively embarked on just such a journey himself in comparable circumstances. And presumably many of the people in that circle were habitual drivers who had known people who had died in car accidents; and presumably all of the circles of the people in their circle fairly bristled with habitual drivers who had known people who had died in car accidents, and so on ad carsicknesseam; such that, as the ratio of drivers to non-drivers in the Occident has visibly not diminished since then and indeed seems to have stood at a steady ten to one since the invention of the Model T, I cannot but conclude that the post-ca. 1920s Occident insgesamt is largely populated by people who drive thousands of hours each year in the very teeth of the most brutally palpable evidence that driving is a lethal activity. As a general policy I eschew the terminology of the pseudo-science of modern psychology like a jumbo jar of bubonic-plague gummies, but I cannot deny that it contains a term that seems to designate the collective post-ca. 1920 Occident's attitude to driving to a non-automotive turn--viz., mass psychosis. In other words, I cannot but conclude that the modern Occidental's complacency towards his own auto-automotive activity is a historically anomalous form of madness. And I am not exactly the first person to have concluded this, for back in 1949, a literary scholar with the impossibly colorful name of Oliver Lawson Dick included in the preface to his edition of the seventeenth-century English biographer John Aubrey's Brief Lives the following remark: "[T]he sword was still [i.e., in Aubrey's time] a part of everyday dress and this made them, when they were in drink, verie apt to doe bloody mischief. However, we soon become indifferent to the lethal weapons of our own times, and there is no reason to think that the danger of stabbing worried the men of the seventeenth century any more than the prospect of a motoring accident troubles us. In fact, the attitude was remarkably similar." The "exactly" in the last sentence of my own is not merely rhetorical, for my concurrence with Mr. or Dr. Dick is merely approximate. Yes, he creditably recognizes that motor-vehicles kill, but en revanche in employing the "democratic 'we'" he shews that he has fallen prey to the general contagion of meta-automotive complacency himself. Moreover, his employment of the phrase "the lethal weapons of our times" betrays his adherence to a decidedly dubious philosophy of history, or at least of the history of lethal weaponry; it shews him to be a sort of extra-meta-scientific Thomas Kuhn avant la lettre according to whom every age has a lethal weapon all its own and either ignored by or unbeknownst to previous and succeeding ages; and that at the end of every age there is a sudden "paradigm shift" wherein everybody suddenly swaps out the weapon he has thitherto been reflexively wielding for the next weapon in the armory (whose contents I am envisioning as arranged like so my shirts and trouser-pairs hanging in a wardrobe or closet, with the wearer moving from the leftmost-hanging shirt and trouser-pair towards the rightmost-hanging shirt and trouser-pair with each change of clothes), which he will thereupon wield with no less mindless aplomb--as if at no point in any given age it had occurred to anybody that that age's weapon was lethal or that the next age's weapon would be. But surely it was and is "a bit more complicated than that," not to mention a bit more rational than that. Surely we do not have no reason to believe that the men of the seventeenth century were worried about the danger of stabbing, for indeed we have every reason to believe that at least one man of the seventeenth century, Aubrey's slight acquaintance Samuel Pepys, was worried thereabout, for he not infrequently wrote of his avoidance of walking home after dark without a "link boy"--i.e., a torchbearer--for fear of being set upon and eviscerated by some opportunistic and fearless cutpurse. And while I suppose it is possible to dismiss Pepys as a cowardly outlier, it would seem that over the course of the remainder of the century (the Diary dating, incidentally, from the 1660s) an ever-crescent proportion of respectable Englishmen shared in his cowardice, for by the second decade of the following century one finds Joseph Addison decrying sword-duels in the Spectator, and by 1730 at the latest the sword has been relegated to a mere fashion accessory worn on only the most formal ceremonial occasions, a weapon with which a gentleman would never dream of girding himself for a routine visit to the coffeehouse, counting-house, or tavern. (Not that one-off physical contests of honor were off the table, but that they were now handled via the sword's less life-threatening replacement, the walking-stick or cudgel, as when Samuel Johnson thrashed an impertinent bookseller to within many comfortable dozens of inches of his life). Clearly at some point at about the turn of the seventeenth to the eighteenth century a general consensus had been reached that the cons of quotidian sword-wearing outweighed the pros, that if men were apt to do bloody mischief to each other when in drink, that mischief ought never to be so mischievous as to extend to the slashing of vital veins and arteries. And so in the present essay I am essentially aiming to do for the cause of forbearance from car-driving what Addison did for the cause of forbearance from sword-dueling. Speaking of forbearance, I have forborne (from) inserting the semi-obligatory "in my own small way" after the immediately preceding "do" first because I do not know to what extent Addison contributed to rendering sword-dueling off-limits among gentlemen with his Spectator piece because for all I know he was therein merely joining an already extant silent majority of anti-sword-duellers; and second(ly), for all I know thanks to the magic of the interweb the present essay will go if not exactly viral than at least (but "at least" sorely undersells the wished-for phenomenon, as you will very shortly see) fungal--which is to say, for all I know it may very slowly, over years if not decades, acquire many millions of readers, in much the same manner as certain funguses in the wild have been known eventually to spread so far that they exceed the some of the world's largest countries in surface area (such that, I believe, a certain member of the kingdom of fungi holds or until recently held the record for the world's largest living organism, occupying as it does or did a goodly portion of the subcutaneal surface of Canada). I realize that the current "discourse" is already inhabited by a massive toadstool of a case against car-driving on the grounds of its contribution to so-called climate change, but my polemic will not constitute an extension or aggravation of that case, not because I do not believe that that contribution is negligible (although I do believe that; or, rather, I believe that climate change is not worth giving a hoot about even if it is occurring at the rate the most lugubrious or hysterical climate doomsayers say it is [my reasons for believing that this hoot is not worth giving being a proverbial "topic for a separate essay" {the integrity of the polar icecaps permitting my getting round to penning it, of course}]) but because my objections to driving will be as fierce as they are today even if I happen to be fortunate enough to live into an epoch in which all automotive vehicles are fully electrified by electricity sourced from fully "renewable" power sources with carbon-emission scores (or whatever the blasted figures are called) of zero. I think that the very existence of any sort of fully-manuverable human-powered vehicle capable of moving discernably faster than the fastest horse is an outrage in itself, and I pine for the days before the invention of such a vehicle was even a twinkle in the eye of Herr Benz or whoever else's eye it actually first twinkled in (for of course, per the online reference work of first resort, Herr Benz was merely the inventor of the "modern car" of which there were presumably prototypes extending all the way back to the days when Archimedes was but a twinkle in his dad's eye; and for aught I know Herr Benz never envisaged his horseless buggy going any discernably faster than a horsed one, such that blame is actually to be laid at the feet of whoever invented the first modern racing car [and naturally I cannot be arsed even to scroll through the online reference work of first resort's article on "car" long enough to learn a single facteme about the above-presumed prototypes, let alone to leap over to the article on "racing car" to learn the name or names of that infernal contraption's inventor or inventors]). Of course it is none too likely that Herr Benz. aut al. ever envisaged the automobile as any sort of contraption that differed from its horse-drawn counterpart only in being guided directly by the ex-rein-bearer, for by his day (or, more properly [exclusively per the terms of the quasi-metaphor, natch] the sub-day thereof on which the aforementioned twinkle began to scintillate) the entire Occident along with a goodly proportion of the Orient had long since developed a mania for speed (the phenomenon of pre-nuclear physics, not the drug) thanks to the invention and relentless improvement of the locomotive train. And this mention of that engine (and, by implication, its appendant wagons) will doubtless cause the magpie-memoried reader to think he has caught me out in a contradiction; for did I not admit many pages ago that I traveled to and from Baltimore and Tampa dozens of times over no fewer dozens of years via train? And does this collection of locomotively actuated journeys not mark me as one of those piners for the golden age of train travel qua infinitely more civilized epoch than the present one? It is, I own, all too easy to suppose reflexively that it does, for the Venn diagram-circles denoting the set of dedicated train travelers and professional piners for the golden age of train travel, respectively, are almost exactly coextensive, but there is a tiny sliver of a semicircle of the former circle, and that sliver is doubtless occupied exclusively by the set of persons named Douglas Robertson, born on April 28, 1972, for unlike every other dedicated train traveler I have ever heard of, I do not yearn to be living within the roughly eighty-year span separating the invention of the train from the invention of the automobile. It is true that I regard that span as a more civilized one than the hundred-and-thirty-year span that immediately succeeded it, but only as a relatively more civilized one; for in my view the dramatic acceleration of travel speed introduced by the locomotive train is (or was) quite barbarous as it is (or was). The truth is that at least in the matter of travel (for I am very much at loggerheads with him on many other matters, notably the recreational use of narcotics) I am very much a spiritual brother of Thomas De Quincey, that I have opted for the train as but the plus mal of pis allers , that I travel (or used to travel) by train because there is no more sluggish alternative available save, perhaps (i.e., if one averages all the hours in which the vehicle races along at the usual ten miles above the speed limit on the interstate and thereby leaves every parallel-vectored Amtrak train trailing in its slipstream [at least everywhere south of the so-called Acela corridor, below which corridor all the trains are diesel-powered and hence seldom manage to exceed the "double-nickels" speed limit of the 1970s and 80s] with the even more numerous hours in which it is crawling along some county or country road with a speed limit of 30 MPH) the long-distance bus. No-stroke-yessiree: if I had my druthers I would never, under any circumstances, allow myself to be transported at more than double my own jogging speed. Why, I recall that as a bairn I would more than occasionally reel with terror even at the sight of the passing asphalt and foliage from the window of the "iconic" yellow bus (a full-length one, mind you!) that transported me to school--and apart from the tractor there is surely no motorized vehicle more dyspeptically fabled for sluggishness than the "iconic" yellow school bus, the bane of pre-rush-hour commuters as it complacently slouches to a halt every hundred feet to disgorge another bunch of tots and via the unfolding of its peremptory portable STOP sign compels every driver in its (non-locomotive) train to sit patiently in place on pain of half-life imprisonment (not to mention full-life ostracism); and it is only by averting my eyes from the window altogether that I can (or could) more than figuratively stomach the barely subsonic speeds attained by those Acela-corridor trains (yes, I know that the Acela is a veritable tortoise compared to a proper high-speed train of the European sort, but at peak speed it is still a mite faster than the average passenger car, and that is far too fast for me [and, incidentally, yet another strike against the anciently bankrupt {yet unkillably "Undead"} notion of Europe qua eternally infungible repository of civilized Occidental tradition {and, sub-incidentally, one of the milliard reasons I have never had much of a desire to live in Europe}]). And so I have never set my heart's time machine to any year later than 1830 qua very last year in which I could have counted on not feeling the faintest waftings of "peer pressure" to take a ride on a train. But even as I begin to re-pine for the golden age of the stage coach, I can feel the wafting of the winged horseless chariot of statistic-quoting naysayers at my back. Upon their imminent arrival these naysayers will say that even if in my beloved "so-called golden age" I would have been spared the anxiety occasioned by super-equine celerity, I would en revanche have been subjected to all sorts of other equally harrowing and harrying somatic shocks that were either peculiar to my preferred mode of transport or not shared with locomotive automotive transport but also administered with even greater ferocity by stage-coach travel. They will doubtless for starters cite some supposedly dead-certain statistics that the accident rate of the stagecoach at its early-nineteenth-century pinnacle of safeness was upteen-hundred times as high as that of the passenger automobile at its mid-twentieth century pre-Naderian nadir of unsafenesss; and that the fatality rate among stage-coach drivers and passengers at that selfsame pinnacle was just as many times as high as that among automobile drivers and passengers at that selfsame nadir. I can't imagine that these boffins have any sound empirical grounds for adducing such figures, for it is not as if there exists some sort of massive paper database on horse-drawn vehicle accidents extending all the way back to Biblical times (although I suspect that something of that sort exists vis-à-vis the last decade or so of the stagecoach age, by which time the mania for integrated data was already in full swing [a mania prefigured by an allusion to proposals for a Universal Register, a pairing of the desiderata of the newly invented help-wanted ad with a sort of pre-telephonic telephone directory, in one of Samuel Johnson's mid-eighteenth-century periodical essays]). Howbeit, I am fully braced for such an arithmetical onslaught, because I know that there is no historical event or phenomenon, be it ever so ancient and be there ever so little surviving documentation of it, for which the statistics Whigs have not got a list of figures as detailed and extensive as this evening's stock-market report, that they will contrive to fabricate an entire National Archives-sized archive out of a two-inch-square fragment of cuneiform or hieroglyphics; and fortunately I am not only braced for it but also armed and armored with what I believe to be the closest thing to an empirically solid data set that we can draw on in this matter--namely, the tens of thousands of pages and several million words hailing from the age of horse-drawn travel that I have read over the past circa 45 years. This corpus is a sort of negative epistemological counterpart of the above-mentioned circle of personally known car-drivers. You see, over the past four to five decades, I have read so many novels and plays set in the age of horse-drawn travel, so many letters and diaries written therein, and so many essays and newspaper-articles and chronicles about people who lived therein, that I can in a none too licentious manner of speaking say that my circle of acquaintances hailing from the age of horse-drawn travel is far more extensive than my circle of acquaintances hailing from the age of automobile travel. And just as I can confidently say that I have known at least eight people who have died in an automobile accident, I can confidently say that I have not known anyone who died in a horse accident or horse-drawn-carriage accident; and this should count as a pretty clear indicator that death by horse-drawn carriage was not at all common in the pre-automotive super-era because I have virtually known an awful lot of people who were obliged to travel by stagecoach, carriage, and horse-cart. The closest approach to such a death that I can recall is an incident recorded in Samuel Johnson's biography of the early eighteenth-century poet Alexander Pope. Pope, Johnson tells us, was riding in across a bridge in a private carriage when the entire equipage, horse, driver, buggy, and passenger tumbled into the deep and icy river waters below. The driver had to (or chose to [No lily-livered Teddie Kennedy-type he!]) smash in one of the windows to pull the poet to the surface, and he consequently lost a finger or two. But Pope turned out to be none the worse for the capsizing-cum--plunge--which is saying something, when one considers that he was a delicately built four-foot-tall hunchback. Oh, yes: and in the Prussian Ludwig Tieck's late-1790s novel Peter Lebrecht there is a description of a carriage accident from which the passenger escapes with no injuries to anything but his patience, although the driver ends up with a cut on his hand--a cut that we may assume is not fatal, inasmuch as after receiving it he manages to convey his passenger to a destination dozens of leagues' distance from the scene of the accident. So from the fact that these two incidents are the direst carriage-accident scenarios I can scare up out of my mental repository of the entire horse-powered era, I gather that death by carriage-accident was quite a rare occurrence. "But," the magpie-eyed reader (who is not necessarily a statistics Whig) will now demur, "your descriptions of these very incidents betray a fact about the horse-drawn era that 'arguably' rendered it a far more hazardous one for travelers than the automotive era--viz., that the carriage-passenger could only sit safely ensconced in his traveling capsule, and that traveling capsule could only get from Point A to Point B, because a horse was pulling it, and because somebody, namely, the driver, was sitting outside the carriage and guiding the horse, and that driver was therefore perforce immediately exposed to any and all concussive surfaces surrounding the equipage in the course of a journey. And from pointing out this morbid oblivion of yours it is an easy transition to reminding you of something I know despite my spotty acquaintance with the archive of the pre-automotive-cum-locomotive era--viz., that not every horse-powered journey involved a vehicle, that a good many people got from Point A to Point B horseback. Indeed, am I not correct in inferring that a tidy majority of the era's journeys were of that nakedly equestrian kind?" You are doubtless right in inferring that across the entire multi-millennial era most journeys were of that kind--although I suspect that by the time stagecoach travel was going at full steam (to employ an admittedly anachronistic and infelicitously train-boosting expression) in the early eighteenth century, most travelers were dedicated carriage-riders who scarcely knew one end of a horse from the other, let alone the difference between a bridle and a snaffle. "Well, then, it seems to me that it's only fair to treat the entire era as our subject-set. And in so doing, to the extent that being enclosed in a vehicle exponentially improves ones chances of being uninjured in an accident, the exact automotive-era analogue to the actual scene of travel in the horse-powered era would be a state of affairs in which our road traffic was dominated by motorcyclists--i.e., in which a huge majority rather than a tiny minority of drivers were exposed to the danger of unenclosed travel." Your analogy, while not impossibly far-fetched, is not at all fair on a number of counts. First of all, it should go without saying that being immediately exposed to the elements while traveling at seventy or eighty miles per hour, as one is easily capable of doing astride even the most retiring of motorcycles, is a good deal more dangerous than being exposed thereto at the twenty-miles-per-maximum that one can attain even astride the fastest of racehorses. And this discrepancy is amply reflected in the folklore on motorcycle accidents vis-à-vis the literature on horse-riding accidents. One all too often hears of a motorcycle accident-victim's pulverized remains' having needed to be "scraped off" whatever surface he and his hoss (or hog) had been so unfortunate as to collide with, whereas one never reads of a horse riding-accident victim's leaving behind a less than fully intact corpse; indeed, descriptions of freshly killed equestrians seldom report any deformity more grotesque than a unintentionally comically lolling head occasioned by a broken neck or a leg twisted uncouthly back against the opposite shoulder in a macabre yet somehow fetching impersonation of one of the more challenging ballet poses. And of course all that extra speed, combined with the intrinsic instability of two wheels as against four hooves makes it all the harder to keep one's balance on a motorcycle than on a horse (yes, I know that the proneness of a two-wheeled vehicle to topple eo ipso diminishes as its speed increases, but en revanche, the faster such a vehicle is going, the faster the rider-driver must react to every disruption of equilibrium), such that even the luckiest of beginning motorcyclists cannot avoid toppling off his vehicle (or toppling while still astride it), whereas even the most hapless of beginning horse-riders rarely finds it difficult to keep his keister planted firmly in the saddle. Finally, and perhaps most fatally, you have anachronistically imposed our era's norm of near-universal drivership on the previous era; you have presupposed that everyone throughout the centuries of horse-powered travel was expected to ride a horse just as virtually everyone is now expected to drive some sort of automotive vehicle, when the truth is that even in the darkest of the Dark Ages when only kings could afford to travel by carriage, a substantial proportion of the respectable Occident could make it from cradle to grave without ever undertaking a journey on horseback, or even learning the barest rudiments of equestrianism. You see, in order to feel the slightest degree of "peer pressure" to learn how to ride a horse, one had to have somewhere to get to with the aid of the animal, and in point of fact most people never had to get to anywhere--at least anywhere not so close by that it would have been more appreciably more expedient to repair thither on horseback than on foot. And even if one did have to make a long-distance journey on foot there was no outright stigma attached to walking thither. Just think of Chaucer's Canterbury pilgrims (apart from the knight, of course, what with horsemanship being the principal job-qualification of knighthood [and the mere fact that horsemanship had an entire profession-cum-social echelon assigned to it shews just how unusual a skill it was]), or J.S. Bach walking from one end of Germany to another just to hear his favorite composer play the organ. "Very well. But you have effectively conceded that even in the darkest of dark ages there were not a few people who needed to get from Point A to Point B at faster than walking speed and that even during the lightest days of the Enlightenment there were such people who could not afford to get therefrom and thereto by wheeled conveyance (here I am of course doing you the favor of bracketing the equine-induced danger faced by the likes of your poor coachmen [not to mention all the unmentioned grooms, stable-hands, et al.] as a marginal occupational hazard)." Indeed I have. "Well, then, you really can't get away with simply writing off horses qua means of personal transportation, and there is one damning fact about horses versus not only motorcycles but all automotive vehicles that you have forgotten (or are pretending to have forgotten); namely, that in contrast to a car, a motorcycle, autc., a horse has got a mind and a will of its own, and a none too rational or affable mind and will at that, such that as soon as it has been let out of the stable (or the starting-gate or what have you) there is no telling what destructive thing it might get it into its head to do at any given moment. A horse may suddenly decide that it doesn't particularly like the chap who's mounted on it and thereupon fling him off its back and onto the ground (where if he does not instantly die in one of your aforementioned positions he still not infrequently suffers an injury that leaves him crippled for life). Or that it doesn't particularly like the chap who's rubbing it down or feeding it its oats and thereupon deliver to him a testicle-pulverizing or front-teeth eliminating kick. Or that a dandelion in the hands of a three-year-old child standing on the side of the road is the scariest thing it has ever seen and thereupon charge at the poor tyke and trample her to death. Again, I admit that I'm a total ignoramus about your pet era, but even I know that back then they were so accustomed to catastrophes of that last kind that they used to put detachable black patches over the sides of horses' eyes by way of preventing them from seeing anything that might "spook" them. And I can't but infer that catastrophes of the first sort were by no means rare because even in our post-equine era one still occasionally hears about them befalling our contemporaries. Just think of Christopher 'Superman' Reeve's last 'tragic' decades as a quadriplegic riding-accident victim." I'll be happy to think about him till the buzzards come home to roost as soon as I have dispatched your objection that the horse is distinguished from a car or a motorcycle in virtue of its possession of a mind and a will. "Are you intimating that My Mother the Car and Christine (the god-awful film adaptation of the god-awful Stephen King novel about the demon-possessed car, not the halfway decent movie about the suicidal anchorwoman [which was of course 'based on a true story']) were in fact not fantastic fictions but meticulously accurate documentaries?" Don't be so crass. No, I am merely intimating that as regards the unpredictability of its inner workings and the potentially disastrous effects thereof, an automotive vehicle might as well have a mind and will of its own. For such a vehicle is such a complicated machine requiring such intricate integration of its thousands of interworking parts that there is no telling what it might "get it into its head" to do while one is nominally in supreme charge of it as a driver, or what catastrophe might befall as a result of this mechanical Einfall. Who among us--an "us" that even includes such anciently long-standing non-drivers such as the present writer--has not felt a sudden plunging of his heart to the depths of terrified despair on seeing that infernal red "engine light" suddenly turn on, and not immediately thereupon launched into a frantic internal debate over whether to continue driving or to pull over to the side of the road and submit to the wallet-hoovering mercies of the American Automobile Association? To be sure, the luminescence of the light may signify nothing more serious than the short-circuiting of a bit of wiring intended to keep it switched off by default; or, if one is slightly less lucky, it will signify some impending serious malfunction whose supervention one can cope with at a comparatively leisurely pace, like my Aries's overheating radiator. But it may signify something far more serious, something that will be many times as effectual at expeditiously turning one into a quadriplegic or cutting short a little girl's life as any whim that might attack the mind of a horse. And of course sometimes the blasted thing doesn't even do one the favor of displaying the warning beforehand. I recall one time backing out of our house's driveway in the Aries just as blithely as can be (or at least as blithely as I could do [which admittedly wasn't very blithely at all]) when one of the rear tie-rods suddenly broke, causing the back of the car to collapse and the appurtenant wheel to stop functioning altogether. I shudder to think what might have happened if that rod had broken only two minutes later, when I would have been hurtling at at least 40 miles per hour around one of the razor-sharp turns of the notoriously serpentine Gunn Highway. At least the Aries was still largely an old-fashioned "analogue" car in which almost every fault was bound to be mechanical in origin, such that even if I couldn't figure out what was wrong with it my father could, he being no mean amateur auto-mechanic (for like most American men of his generation, he had in his youth made a hobby of tinkering with a succession of cars of his own ownership). Nowadays, when the movement of every gear, bolt, latch, etc. of a car is mediated if not directly actuated by an internal computer--such that our psychological metaphor has become quasi-literal, and the cause of every fault all the more inscrutable--the would-have-been amateur mechanic is obliged to hand his ailing vehicle directly over to a professional one, who is turn obliged to double as a computer scientist (or perhaps to serve as a mere middle man between the computer scientist and the customer [I admit I am starting to get over my computerized jet-skis here]). And the Deity alone knows what horrors drivers will face, when, thanks to the "internet of things," "cyber-car-jacking" really gets into full swing. The interweb scuttlebutt has it that the Chinese and the Russians are developing, or possibly have already developed, technology that will allow them to take full remote control of any Occidental vehicle from the comfort of downtown Beijing or Moscow, and thereby "paradoxically" impart to it an all-too-real and human mind-cum-will, that of Mr. Xi or Mr. Putin. In any case, your sub-example featuring the blinders is decidedly ill-placed in an argument against the use of the horse as a self-contained means of self-conveyance; for blinders were applied exclusively to the heads of carriage-drawing vehicles, and specifically to coach horses and the haulers of urban cabs, and even then not even exclusively or mainly for the purpose of forestalling full-blown spookage. You see, by then, people were beginning to place a high premium on getting to where they were going as quickly and efficiently as possible, such that it was vital for the horse's eyes to be kept on the road, yes, to preclude its being distracted from roadside entities, but mainly lest the distraction occasion any slackening of the animal's pace. But even to the extent that spooking is relevant, the danger of it was especially acute in connection with these animals on account of the unusually bewildering, tiring, and distressing circumstances in which they were compelled to labor. It is small wonder that they ended up more than a trifle skittish, a trifle "neurotic," given that they were required to be perpetually on the move and were constantly being shifted from the care and supervision of one temporary human master to another. And this observation reveals that your argument fails to take into account perhaps the chief and most pertinent difference between a car and a horse--namely that unlike a car a horse has a temperament. From my reading I gather that in the old pre-automotive days horses varied widely in temperament and that this diversity was no accident--that, in a word, horses were deliberately bred for specific temperaments. Of course, horses are still bred now, but exclusively for the racetrack, and one tends to think of the breeding of horses in terms directly analogous to those of the breeding of royal human lineages--that there are high hopes for a certain stallion to win the Triple Crown because he is both the great-great-grandson of Secretariat and the great-great-great-nephew of the mare who bore Phar Lap, and so forth. Of course a trace of a less subculturally straitened approach to horse-breeding survives in the draft horse known as the Clydesdale (or at least in the idea thereof that survives in the memories of American beer-drinkers of a certain age [for I can't recall the last time I saw a Clydesdale featured in a Budweiser commercial {and I am strongly inclined to suppose that the Budweiser Clydesdales have long since gone the way of the Ringling Brothers Circus's elephants thanks to the misguided "empathy" of animal-rights activists}]). But my impression is that in the old days this approach was as expansive in the domain of horse-breeding as it still is now in the domains of domestic pet-breeders; that just as the world now abounds with breeds of cats and dogs tailored to the wildly heterogeneous desiderata of pet-owners (or "pet-guardians" or even "pet-parents" as one must now call them), so the world then abounded with breeds of horses tailored to then still wildly heterogeneous desiderata of horse-riders. We now have a sub-breed of pit bull bred to pounce on the throat of the first human infant to look at it crosswise by way of gratifying the owner's perverse desire to browbeat his neighbors for not having instilled in their child the etiquette of human-canine eye-contact in utero (this by further way of gratifying his conceit that his dog is by nature a perfect angel that wouldn't hurt a fly provided the insect deferentially dipped its antennae to him before alighting on his nose); we now have a breed of cat--I believe it is known as a "ragdoll"--guaranteed to collapse into a heap of invertebrate fur in the hands of total strangers by way of gratifying its owner's wholesome if hyper-quixotic craving for a living sublunary example of unconditional trust. So in the old days they bred hunting-horses so eager to give the hounds a run for their money in the fox-chase that their kamikaze-en-avant-la lettre riders were fain to update their last wills-and-testaments on the eve of every hunt and to chug a jeroboam of brandy on the morning thereof; and they also bred horses whose gaits were so gentle, whose hoof-falls were so lightly percussive, that the most brittle-bone nonagenarian widows would not hesitate to ride them to church. They even had names for specific types of horses bred for specific types of riders and styles of riding. A horse "for ordinary riding, especially women" was known as a palfrey (per my eighth edition [1990] Concise Oxford Dictionary, which labels the word archaic, and I can vouch for both its archaism and its ancient commonness, for it was an occurrence of it in some eighteenth or early nineteenth-century text that sent me looking it up in that selfsame dictionary. And in the aforementioned Peter Lebrecht, a certain character by profession a bailiff, a kind of rural civil servant, boasts of having chosen as his mode of transport on his daily rounds through the countryside a horse designated by a word Englished as "ambler" and that "moved with such a gentle step that one could read a book from cover to cover while riding it." One cannot but assume that it was an ambler astride which Erasmus did the thousands of pages of essential reading in philosophy and theology that he reports having accomplished in journeying across Northern Europe on horseback. But seriously, Volker, can you get over what a boon of a convenience it must have been to be able to read while driving? Yes, I know we have so-called audio books aplenty nowadays, and I know that people are now forever boasting of having "read" the likes of War and Peace and the complete works of Shakespeare over the course of a coast-to-coast road trip, but the honest-to-goodness truth is that they have no more read these books and corpora than they have seen the myriad towns and burgs they have zipped past during such a trip, for the aitch-tee-gee truth is that automotive driving is far too attention-exacting an activity to allow one to absorb any sort of intellectual content more intricate or extensive than the daily news or weather report. As for automotive riding, it must be owned that even at its most distracting--i.e., when one is standing "Japanese-subway" style shoulder-to-shoulder with a full busload of fellow-travelers--it allows one to retreat more or less fully into one's own thoughts or fully concentrate one's attention on a sustained and complicated piece of reading matter. I can in all good faith assert that I read a great deal of substance indeed during my bus commutes, because I remember many of the titles of the books I scanned with my eyes while on an MTA bus, and I can now recall the contents of those books as readily as I can any of the books that I read in the (relatively) quiet environs of my apartment. And so I am herewith brought full circle to my guarded apologia for bus-ridership; brought, specifically, to a respectably if not quite eminently practicable defense of it qua pis aller for our recovery of the phenomenology of travel during the horse-powered era. Yes, it is true that even during a typical municipal bus commute one is still traveling faster than is good for one's somatic well-being--but only intermittently and even then not much faster. Moreover, during a bus ride the abovementioned anxiety engendered by the imagination of a prospective crash vanishes almost into nonexistence: one does not much worry what will happen if the bus collides with another vehicle because one supposes that ninety-nine times out of a hundred the bus will be by far the larger of the two vehicles and will consequently suffer almost no damage in consequence of colliding with it. One feels an almost vicarious sense of triumph at the prospect of the bus's reducing some hapless compact into accordionized scrap metal while suffering not a scratch itself. In my case this easeful feeling has been abetted by my being involved in at least two such accidents--one on the 61, at 28th or 29th street in Charles Village, on my way into work, the other on the Number 11 on some cross street in downtown Towson, on my way from work to my first attendance of an after-work "meetup" (for which I see in hindsight I should have taken the accident as a drolly ill omen, the "meetup" being that of a self-styled German "Stammtisch" purportedly dedicated to its members' practicing of their conversational German but actually dedicated to the airing of their various but uniformly banal beeves in quasi-monological English). In both cases the aftermath was far more "traumatic" than the accident itself and was entirely occasioned by the behavior of the driver--in the first case, her (a white woman's) skittish efforts to direct the other driver's disengagement from her left flank [for the accident had been a "side-swipe" from the left lane; the second, her (she being a stroppy, nearly clinically obese black woman's) dogged refusal to grant us "survivors" permission to leave the bus, for upon the departure of a young white Baltimore County police officer's smilingly polite yet impeccably efficient interrogation of each of us (he having asked each of us to describe what had happened whether we were unhurt, and if not, whether we planned to file any sort of insurance claim), she simply sat morosely awaiting the arrival of the MTA officer who would collect her from the scene (and doubtless nursing nightmarish fantasies about the consequences of this black mark [!] for her subsequent career), thereby engendering in my own mind fantasies about being towed away along with the vehicle. In the first accident nothing but a low crunching noise alerted me to the fact that it was taking place; in the second I felt nothing worse than a slight jolt that roused me from my readerly meditations (even if a pronounced ache that I felt in my left elbow for several days in succession afterwards proved to me that I had not escaped entirely without injury). The virtual invincibility of buses seems to account for the fact that even the abovementioned yellow school buses are bereft of passenger safety belts.

All "that said, I realize that for all but a minuscule fraction of us Americans the regular use of public bus transit is an impossibility; that only a minuscule fraction of us live within ten minutes' walking distance of a bus stop, but there is really no reason apart "a lack of political will" to prevent the proportions from being inverted, and the filling of that lack is largely contingent on my fellow nice, normal Americans' overcoming of the above-mentioned squeamishness about the loucheness of typical bus riders, which squeamishness would of course vanish as a matter of course in a "cascade preference" upon the development of a "critical mass" of nice, normal bus riders. I own that the preceding "largely" is a rather tentative "largely," because of course nobody ever actually publicly says that he would never ride a bus on account of the loucheness of the ridership, everybody tending instead to adduce a more impersonal argument founded on a less politically incendiary basis, and I balk at unceremoniously assuming that this publicly avowed argument is a mere stalking horse or fig leaf for undisclosed snobbery. I am thinking here of the argument that bus systems with decent coverage are fundamentally impracticable in most American metropolitan areas because these areas are too "low-density" to support a sizable ridership. But my long experience as a regular bus rider in the Baltimore metropolitan area and keen observer (from the window of my passenger's seat in other people's cars) of the street-level operations and infrastructure of the Tampa metropolitan sub-area's HART bus system has pretty generously given the lie to this argument. It is true that the city of Baltimore is substantially more densely populated than the city of Tampa (although even that discrepancy is growing ever narrower as Baltimore continues reliably to hemorrahage residents [its population having diminished from about 750,000 when I moved there in 1994, to about 590,000 today] and Tampa continues inexorably to absorb them [its population having grown from about 270,000 to 340,000 in the same interval]). But even Baltimore is possessed of fully urban density only at its ca. 15 square-mile-core; once one reaches the numbered streets in the low twenties (heading north from the center along Howard Street, as I occasionally did on, I believe, the Number 27) a mere two miles north of the dead center of downtown, one finds oneself in a semi-suburban landscape reminiscent of midtown Tampa, with the rows of row-houses yielding pride of place to embryonic strip-malls, filling stations, and abandoned or repurposed used-car dealerships. When one gets to the northern city limits via the trajectory of the old Number 3, 8, or 11, the geography has become downright outer Temple-Terrace-like, with the streetside landscape being dominated by fully grown strip malls (including their several acre-sized parking lots), garden-apartment complexes (i.e., the type in which the housing is apportioned among a dozen or so clusters of two-story buildings clustered around tiny parking lots), and patches of uncleared woodlands. And by the time one gets to Cockeysville, nominally an outer inner suburb, one feels that one is fast approaching the proper sticks, for there entire street blocks of woodland separate isolated one-story commercial establishments from each other. And yet Cockeysville is--or until recently was--serviced at least hourly by an MTA bus, the Number 8 (or its color-coded replacement). By the same (or, rather, a complementary) token, one can most definitely take a HART bus to Temple Terrace, and I have very recently seen HART bus stops alongside portions of Gunn Highway not seven miles south of my house here in nominally semi-rural Keystone, stretches flanked by garden apartment complexes and supermarket-anchored strip malls, with scarcely a tree or non-functional shrubbery in sight. So it is plainly possible to cover an entire large-ish American conurbation, outer suburbs and all, with a network of buses; and plain to the eyes indeed that an allegedly ridiculously low-density such conurbation such as Tampa's is already being covered thereby. And yet Baltimore's MTA bus-transit system is--or at least until very recently was--one that is famously usable, whereas Tampa's HART bus-transit system always has been (i.e., very much still is) a byword for uselessness. The salient and indeed fatal difference between the two systems inheres not in the size or range of its geographical footprint but in the number of buses, the frequency of their visits to each stop, and (perhaps most saliently and indeed fatally) the extensiveness of the schedule, the MTA being a twenty-four-hour, seven-days-a-week system and HART being one whose schedule commences at daybreak and closes at 8:00 or 9:00 pm even on weekdays, such that it is fundamentally ill-suited to any demographic type but the commuter to and from a traditional nine-to-five office job (yes, yes, yes: the very demographic type legendarily least inclined to take up bus ridership). One can, and indeed one did, find oneself taking an MTA bus to visit a friend or see a movie on a Saturday or Sunday, whereas one would never dream of using a HART bus for such an excursion. But the infrastructure for turning the HART system into a serviceable (if admittedly hardly spectacular) public-transit provider is already in place; all that is needed to realize the transformation is a substantial increase in the size of its fleet and driver-force. Of course this will or would cost a fair amount of money, and the raising of that money will or would require the levying of additional taxes, and so what is so far lacking is merely the above-mentioned political will. And of course the boilerplate knee-jerk response to this observation is that Hillsborough County, merely in virtue of being sited in Florida, is inhabited by a hyper-individualistic citizenry who would sooner be hanged (or, in present-day terms, electrocuted) than suffer their taxes to be raised by so much as a dime in support of anything so unregenerately collective-minded as a decent public-transportation system, and that this "Tea-Party"-ish disposition has only been reeinforced by the post-COVID metamorphosis of Florida from a so-called purple state into a so-called blue one. And far be it from me to dismiss this rebuttal out of hand merely because it is reflexive and unvarying. All the same, I am inclined to think that its wielders less accurately gauge our current Staat- und Stadtgeist than they suppose, for it is plain to every long-time Hillsborough Countian with eyes wherewith to see (and perhaps more materially, without money to burn) that the just-mentioned purple-to-red Staatsverwandlung has been accompanied (and to a certain extent preceded) by the transformation of the Tampa Bay area from an extremely square or naff quasi-metropolis into a fairly hip or fashionable one. I would place the terminus a quo of the metamorphosis at the opening of the Armature Works complex a couple of miles north of downtown Tampa about a dozen years ago. One could regard that complex either as Tampa's extremely belated answer to Baltimore's Inner Harbor or as its extremely timely answer to Manhattan's Chelsea Market. The "blueprint" for such projects--take some abandoned industrial structure of supposed local-historical significance, gut it, and fill it with modern retail establishments is very much "one size fits all," but the "vibe" of each project is quite micro-epocally specific. For example, Tampa had a very small-scale realization of the "blueprint" as early as the mid-1970s (i.e., even before the Inner Harbor's official christening as an entertainment zone in ca. 1980) in Ybor Square, whose name belied its location in a single former cigar factory occupying but a single side of its quadrilateral eponym. The retail establishments were less like shops than booths, and the goods for sale there were not much more respectable than the sort one would encounter at a flea market--old but scarcely ever rare books, old but by no means "antique" furniture, postcards and family snapshots from the early twentieth century, and the like, the lot more than figuratively exuding a musty smell of decay. In short its "vibe" was unreconstructedly old-school bohemian in a very 1970s sort of way, pitched by and at people who didn't have much money to spend and who were undiscriminating aficianodos of the bygone and offbeat rather than proper connoisseurs and collectors. The Inner Harbor, by contrast, has been called "Disneyesque" in virtue of its sprawling size, its retail domination by kid-friendly entities like fudge factories and retro-1950s hamburger joints, the cluttering of its interstitial bits by rambling jugglers and cotton-candy vendors, its inclusion of and by a skating rink, the didactic Maryland Science Center, the Port Discovery children's museum, etc. But there is nothing either old-school bohemian or Disneyesque about Chelsea Market or the Armature Works, where even the most downmarket restaurants are upmarket "gastropubs," and not a few are Michelin Star-seeking servers of regional cuisine of the most recherché regions, and the shop-shelves are exclusively occupied by high-priced "artisanal" goods whose collectability has been pre-vetted. In short, these new developments are aiming for the wallets of well-to-do retired Boomers and childless Gen-Xers and Millennials (or, vis-à-vis the last generational demographic, the minority thereof who do not prefer to lavish their disposable income on "cosplay"-themed excursions to Disney World [all the while that the Inner Harbor "ironically" languishes in moribundity, its Disneyesqueness powerless to save it for want of the imprimatur of the Mouse himself]); theirs is a hipness with decidedly yuppie-ish characteristics (or, rather, characteristics that would be called yuppie-ish if "we" weren't all yuppies now). But the Armature Works is but one station on the urban pilgrim's route known as the Riverwalk, an eight-mile promenade along the Hillsborough River commencing in downtown Tampa, specifically at a sort of plaza housing the Tampa Museum of Art and, in winter, an artificial ice-skating rink (admittedly but a puny simulacrum of its naturally chilly northern-urban counterparts, but in such a super-subtropical environment it is surely the thought that counts) and ending in the immediate vicinity of the Lowry Park Zoo (perennially ranked as "one of the top-ten zoos in the United States" as measured by some ineffable extra-zoological criterion like kid-friendliness). The Riverwalk reminds me most strongly of Lower Manhattan's sickeningly trendy urban nature walk known as the High Line, and for aught I know it was inspired thereby, although for my money (or lack thereof [for so far one can at least peregrinate the Riverwalk itself for free]) it makes for a much more agreeable session of flaneurage than the High Line owing to its abundance of elbow-room (for despite all the greenery, the High Line is as crowded with pedestrian traffic as any Greenwich-Village sidwalk), and I can readily imagine that many a Manhattanite would agree with me--nay, that many a visitor from the Big Apple has already breathlessly praised its superiority to the High Line to his fellow New Yorkers. Downtown is also the western terminus of the single line of the TECO Streetcar, Tampa's very belated answer to San Francisco's cable cars (and even vaguely timely answer to no "urban redevelopment" project that I can recall). Along this single line a bright and shiny replica of one of the electric trams that trundled along Tampa's streets by the dozens in the very early twentieth century endelssly oscillates between downtown and Ybor City, a half-mile or so to the west. As of now the streetcar system is little more than a toy, a sort of man-sized version of the trolley route connecting Mr. Rogers' house to the Neighborhood of Make-Believe, but I hear that there is not only talk but a full-blown plan of extending and augmenting it into at least one full-blown light-rail line stretching all the way northward to Busch Gardens. The realization of such a project would be bound to cost hundreds of millions of dollars, if not more than a billion. Will the good or not-so-good people of Hillsborough County be persuaded to cough up or fork over the dozen or so-million-dollar portion of those costs that will presumably not be covered by the state or federal government? Quite possibly not. En revanche, I can't imagine such a project's reaching the planning stage "in my day" (i.e., ca. 1980 to 1999), such that we may infer that Hillsborough's tax base is now more abundantly peopled by types receptive thereto, and there are few if any reasons to suppose that it will not be even more abundantly peopled by such types five or ten years hence. And if that tax base will prove amenable to financing such a costly boondoggle, surely it will prove amenable to financing such a comparatively cheap boondoggle as a bigger bus fleet as a matter of course, practically in its sleep, so to speak. Supernumerary Objections and Replies Thereto:

Objection: The "emotional trauma" induced by air travel differs from that induced by automobile travel in one salient respect; namely, that whereas one can avoid the latter altogether by not flying, one cannot avoid even the worst of the latter by not driving, for wherever you go in the present-day Occident, you will find yourself having to be constantly on the for the approach of an automobile, and to be shifting your position at a few seconds' notice . Ergo, you might as well start driving again--nay, you might very well even reduce your meta-automotive trauma by resuming driving, inasmuch as you would thereby be reducing the proportion of your hours spent as a pedestrian, and as everyone knows, one is much less likely to die when struck by an automobile while encased in an automotive exoskeleton than when struck by one as "a bare forked animal."

Reply: First of all, it is patently untrue that one can avoid airline travel-induced trauma by not flying, inasmuch as an apparent "overwhelming majority" of air crashes occur in the first few minutes after takeoff or during the aircraft's descending approach to the runway, and owing to the propinquity of airports even to the outermost suburbs of most metropolitan areas it is practically impossible to avoid exposing oneself to being an on-the-ground victim of one of these crashes. The best that one can do is select a residence that is out of the flight-paths of all the major airlines, but even if one manages to do this one's security is bound to be short-lived, as these flight-paths are constantly changing. At the moment from my house I seem to behold airliners ascending and descending only in the near distance, but I can recall a time when I would see them directly overhead, and as a college student I was obliged to live directly next door to Sarasota-Bradenton International Airport for four straight years during which the never-dispersing stench of jet-fuel exhaust and the seldom sub-deafening roar of aircraft engines were ever-present reminders of my potentially imminent aviational annihilation.

Objection: As you have admitted that you live in "the sticks" I gather that you are beyond the reach of the HART bus system, from which I further gather that you are not infrequently obliged to exploit the driving of others in your capacity as their passenger, and thereby quite hypocritically exposing yourself, and rather heartlessly exposing them, to a more than trivial extra quantum of automotive peril.

Reply: This is a more serious objection than the first one, but I believe I am capable of answering it if not in full then at least in massive part. It is indeed true that I now reside in a neighborhood or "community" beyond the reach of the HART bus system, but as unequivocally indicated far above, I did not voluntarily relocate hither from a residence well within reach of Baltimore's MTA bus system. "That said," I own that upon finding myself in such a bus-forsaken named place, I was and remain obliged to do my darndest to re-relocate to a bus-blessed one, and I have been doing at least my darnder almost from Day One of my residence in the locale. The chief obstacle thereto, of course, has from Day One been of a monetary or financial nature, for I cannot reasonably be expected to attempt such a re-relocation until I enjoy the prospect of a steady income whose monthly portions will allow me to live on my own for the indefinite future in the decidedly modest manner to which I have grown accustomed, and I have so far enjoyed no such prospect. Admittedly this is "arguably" entirely my own fault, but it is also "arguably" the fault of my stars alone, so let us split the difference of blame and simply say that I could have tried and could still stand to try a bit harder to resituate myself into a more pedestrian-friendly neighborhood.
